<Callout type="abstract" title="Unit Overview">

The present simple is the backbone of everyday English. It allows speakers and writers to describe the world as it *consistently is* — not just what is happening at a given moment, but what is reliably, repeatedly, or permanently true. Mastering it fully means going beyond basic conjugation: it means knowing *when* to use it, *when not to*, and how its forms interact with negation, questions, and register.

**By the end of this unit you will be able to:**

- Form the present simple correctly in all persons, including irregular third-person singular spellings
- Construct accurate negatives using *do/does + not + infinitive*
- Form questions using *do/does + subject + infinitive* with appropriate question words
- Distinguish present simple from present progressive using contextual cues and time expressions
- Use the present simple correctly in instructions, commentaries, and stories

</Callout>

## Section A — The Full Form

The present simple has two distinct forms depending on the subject. For most subjects, the verb stays in its base form. But when the subject is *he*, *she*, or *it* (third person singular), the verb takes a special ending.

Think of it like a vending machine: for most buttons (I, you, we, they), the machine gives you the same product. But the *he/she/it* button adds a small wrapper — the *-s* ending — around the same product.

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Subject", "Form", "Example"]}
  rows={[
    ["I", "base verb", "I work in a shared office."],
    ["You", "base verb", "You travel a lot for business."],
    ["He / She / It", "base verb + -s", "She works from home on Fridays."],
    ["We", "base verb", "We collaborate across time zones."],
    ["They", "base verb", "They manage a fully remote team."],
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="note" title="Key Insight">

The *-s* ending only ever appears in the **third person singular** (he/she/it). It never appears with *I*, *you*, *we*, or *they*. This sounds simple, but is one of the most common errors in writing.

</Callout>

## Section B — Third Person Singular: Spelling Rules

The *-s* ending is not always simply added. There are four distinct patterns.

**Pattern 1 — Most verbs: add -s**

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Base Form", "Third Person Singular"]}
  rows={[
    ["work", "works"],
    ["read", "reads"],
    ["play", "plays"],
    ["send", "sends"],
    ["travel", "travels"],
    ["earn", "earns"],
    ["report", "reports"],
    ["design", "designs"],
  ]}
/>

**Pattern 2 — Verbs ending in -s, -sh, -ch, -x, -o: add -es**

These verbs already end in a sound that makes them hard to pronounce with just *-s*, so *-es* is added to create a clear extra syllable.

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Base Form", "Third Person Singular"]}
  rows={[
    ["pass", "passes"],
    ["wash", "washes"],
    ["watch", "watches"],
    ["fix", "fixes"],
    ["go", "goes"],
    ["do", "does"],
    ["miss", "misses"],
    ["push", "pushes"],
    ["teach", "teaches"],
    ["reach", "reaches"],
  ]}
/>

**Pattern 3 — Verbs ending in consonant + y: drop -y, add -ies**

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Base Form", "Third Person Singular"]}
  rows={[
    ["try", "tries"],
    ["fly", "flies"],
    ["carry", "carries"],
    ["study", "studies"],
    ["apply", "applies"],
    ["identify", "identifies"],
    ["rely", "relies"],
    ["deny", "denies"],
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="tip" title="Vowel + y Exception">

When a vowel comes *before* the -y, simply add *-s* (no change): *play → plays*, *stay → stays*, *say → says*, *buy → buys*. The consonant + y rule does NOT apply here.

</Callout>

**Pattern 4 — Irregular forms**

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Base Form", "Third Person Singular"]}
  rows={[
    ["have", "has"],
    ["be", "is"],
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="warning" title="The Most Dangerous Mistake">

Students often write *she have* or *he don't* — forgetting to apply the third-person singular rule to the **auxiliary** as well as the main verb. In negatives and questions, *do* becomes *does* for he/she/it.

</Callout>

## Section C — Negatives: Do Not / Does Not

The present simple negative is formed with *do not* (contraction: *don't*) or *does not* (contraction: *doesn't*) followed by the bare infinitive.

**Key principle:** The *-s* ending migrates from the main verb to the auxiliary. In the negative, the main verb loses its ending.

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Statement", "Negative"]}
  rows={[
    ["She works remotely.", "She does not work remotely."],
    ["He travels every week.", "He does not travel every week."],
    ["They meet on Thursdays.", "They do not meet on Thursdays."],
    ["We use a shared platform.", "We do not use a shared platform."],
    ["The software runs on Linux.", "The software does not run on Linux."],
  ]}
/>

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "She doesn't works from home.", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "She doesn't work from home.", note: "doesn't already carries the third-person marker. The main verb returns to its base form." },
]} />

<Callout type="note" title="Formal vs Informal Register">

In formal writing (reports, academic texts, business correspondence), prefer the full forms: *do not*, *does not*. In conversation and informal writing, contractions (*don't*, *doesn't*) are natural and preferred.

</Callout>

## Section D — Questions: Do / Does + Subject + Infinitive

To form a yes/no question, invert *do/does* and the subject. To form a wh-question, add a question word before *do/does*.

**Yes/No Questions**

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Statement", "Question"]}
  rows={[
    ["You work from home.", "Do you work from home?"],
    ["She manages a team.", "Does she manage a team?"],
    ["They use cloud software.", "Do they use cloud software?"],
    ["He attends meetings.", "Does he attend meetings?"],
  ]}
/>

**Wh-Questions**

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Question Word", "Structure", "Example"]}
  rows={[
    ["What", "What + does + subject + verb?", "What does she do exactly?"],
    ["Where", "Where + do + subject + verb?", "Where do they hold their meetings?"],
    ["When", "When + does + subject + verb?", "When does the shift start?"],
    ["Why", "Why + do + subject + verb?", "Why do companies allow remote work?"],
    ["How", "How + does + it + verb?", "How does the system handle updates?"],
    ["How many", "How many + does + subject + verb?", "How many hours does he work a week?"],
    ["Who", "Who + does + subject + verb?", "Who does she report to?"],
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="warning" title="Word Order Trap">

The subject always comes after *do/does*, not after the main verb. The auxiliary (*does*) inverts with the subject (*she*), not the main verb.

</Callout>

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "Where works she these days?", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "Where does she work these days?", note: "English questions require do/does as the question auxiliary. The main verb does not move forward alone." },
]} />

## Section E — Three Lives of the Present Simple

The present simple performs at least three distinct roles in English.

### Life 1: Habits and Routines

Describing things that happen regularly, repeatedly, or as part of someone's pattern of behaviour. Frequency adverbs (*always, often, usually, sometimes, rarely, never*) are common companions.

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "The marketing director reviews her inbox every morning before 8 a.m." },
  { sentence: "Remote workers often set fixed start and finish times to maintain structure." },
  { sentence: "He rarely commutes to the office — perhaps once a fortnight." },
  { sentence: "They hold a team video call every Monday and Friday." },
]} />

### Life 2: Permanent States and Universal Truths

For facts that are always or generally true — scientific laws, geographical realities, definitions, and stable states of being.

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius at sea level." },
  { sentence: "The company employs over 3,000 people across twelve countries." },
  { sentence: "A fixed contract guarantees a minimum number of working hours per week." },
]} />

### Life 3: Instructions, Demonstrations, and Commentaries

In instructions, live commentary, and informal storytelling, the present simple creates immediacy and forward momentum.

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "First, you open the application and select your workspace.", note: "instruction" },
  { sentence: "Turn left at the crossroads, continue for 200 metres, then take the first exit.", note: "directions" },
]} />

<Callout type="note" title="Academic and Formal Usage">

In academic writing, the present simple is used to state findings, cite authors, and describe processes: *The study shows that remote workers report higher autonomy.* / *Smith (2019) argues that flexibility increases output.* This use is especially important for IELTS and academic writing tasks.

</Callout>

## Section F — Present Simple vs Present Progressive

Both are 'present' tenses, but they answer fundamentally different questions.

**The Present Simple asks:** *What is generally / always / repeatedly true?*

**The Present Progressive asks:** *What is happening right now or around this period?*

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Present Simple (Portrait)", "Present Progressive (Action Shot)"]}
  rows={[
    ["She works remotely. (her regular situation)", "She is working from the library today. (unusual, right now)"],
    ["He checks his email every hour. (routine)", "He is checking his email — don't disturb him. (happening now)"],
    ["They collaborate using shared tools. (general truth)", "They are collaborating on a new proposal this week. (current project)"],
    ["The company runs quarterly reviews. (permanent policy)", "The company is running a trial of flexible hours. (temporary change)"],
  ]}
/>

**Time expressions as signals**

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Present Simple Signals", "Present Progressive Signals"]}
  rows={[
    ["always, usually, often, sometimes", "now, right now, at this moment"],
    ["rarely, never", "currently, at present"],
    ["every day / week / Monday", "these days, this week, this month"],
    ["once a week, twice a year", "still, no longer"],
    ["on Fridays, in the mornings", "for the time being"],
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="tip" title="The Changing World Exception">

Use the present progressive (not simple present) to describe trends and changes in progress — even if they are slow: *Remote work **is becoming** more widespread.* / *The economy **is shifting** towards digital services.* These changes are still in motion, so the progressive captures them better.

</Callout>

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "I work on a big proposal this week — it takes all my time.", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "I am working on a big proposal this week — it is taking all my time.", note: "'This week' signals a current, temporary activity — the progressive is needed for both verbs." },
]} />

## Section G — Non-Progressive Verbs: State Verbs

A significant group of verbs almost never appears in the present progressive. These are called **state verbs** because they describe states of mind, feelings, possession, perception, or logical relationships rather than actions.

**Mental and emotional states:** *believe, know, understand, think (= have an opinion), realise, suppose, doubt, imagine, remember, forget, recognise*

**Feelings and preferences:** *like, love, hate, prefer, want, need, wish*

**Perception (with can):** *can see, can hear, can smell, can feel, can taste*

**Possession and existence:** *have (= possess), own, belong, contain, consist of, include*

**Other common state verbs:** *seem, appear, mean, matter, depend, cost, fit, weigh, measure*

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Wrong — Progressive Form", "Correct — Simple Form"]}
  rows={[
    ["I am knowing the answer.", "I know the answer."],
    ["She is wanting a promotion.", "She wants a promotion."],
    ["He is believing the report.", "He believes the report."],
    ["The contract is containing a penalty clause.", "The contract contains a penalty clause."],
    ["I am preferring the second option.", "I prefer the second option."],
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="warning" title="Verbs That Can Be Both">

Some verbs have two meanings — one is a state (use simple), one is an action (use progressive).

</Callout>

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Verb", "State Meaning (Simple)", "Action Meaning (Progressive)"]}
  rows={[
    ["think", "I think you're right. (= believe)", "I am thinking about the offer. (= considering)"],
    ["have", "She has a corner office. (= possesses)", "We are having a meeting. (= experiencing)"],
    ["see", "I see what you mean. (= understand)", "I am seeing the HR director tomorrow. (= meeting)"],
    ["look", "That report looks excellent. (= seems)", "She is looking at the data right now. (= directing gaze)"],
    ["feel", "I feel this approach is wrong. (= believe)", "He is feeling better today. (= physical state)"],
  ]}
/>

## Section H — Register Awareness

**Informal / Spoken English:**
- Contractions are standard: *don't*, *doesn't*
- Short question tags are natural: *She works from home, doesn't she?*
- Simple vocabulary in habitual contexts: *He gets up early, makes coffee, and starts work.*

**Formal / Written English:**
- Full negative forms preferred: *The policy does not apply to contractors.*
- Third-person singular in statements of fact: *The data suggests a clear correlation.*
- Academic present simple for citing: *The author demonstrates that... / Research confirms that...*

<Callout type="tip" title="Register Checkpoint">

If you are writing an IELTS Task 2 essay or a formal report, avoid contractions. Write *does not* and *do not* throughout. In Speaking Part 1 or an informal email, contractions are natural and expected.

</Callout>

## Section I — 12 Classic Mistakes

### Mistake 1 — Missing -s in third person singular

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "She work from home every day.", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "She works from home every day.", note: "Third person singular always needs -s in the present simple." },
]} />

### Mistake 2 — Adding -s with does in questions

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "Does she works remotely?", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "Does she work remotely?", note: "does already carries the third-person marker; the main verb returns to its base form." },
]} />

### Mistake 3 — Using don't instead of doesn't

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "He don't attend the Friday briefing.", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "He doesn't attend the Friday briefing.", note: "He/she/it requires doesn't, not don't." },
]} />

### Mistake 4 — Wrong spelling after -ch

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "He teachs the new graduates.", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "He teaches the new graduates.", note: "Verbs ending in -ch take -es: teach → teaches." },
]} />

### Mistake 5 — Forgetting -y to -ies change

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "The system identifys the user automatically.", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "The system identifies the user automatically.", note: "Consonant + y → drop y, add -ies: identify → identifies." },
]} />

### Mistake 6 — Present simple for a current temporary activity

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "I work on a proposal this week — it's very complex.", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "I am working on a proposal this week — it's very complex.", note: "'This week' signals a temporary current activity → present progressive." },
]} />

### Mistake 7 — State verb in the progressive

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "I am knowing that she is right.", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "I know that she is right.", note: "know is a state verb and cannot be used in the progressive." },
]} />

### Mistake 8 — Wrong question word order

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "Where works the remote team usually?", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "Where does the remote team usually work?", note: "Question structure: Wh-word + do/does + subject + base verb." },
]} />

### Mistake 9 — Double negative

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "She never doesn't miss a deadline.", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "She never misses a deadline.", note: "English uses one negative element only. never + affirmative verb; OR doesn't + base verb." },
]} />

### Mistake 10 — Present simple for duration up to now

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "I work here since 2019.", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "I have worked here since 2019.", note: "Duration up to now requires the present perfect, not the present simple." },
]} />

### Mistake 11 — Progressive for a universal truth

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "Water is boiling at 100 degrees Celsius.", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius.", note: "Scientific and universal truths use the present simple." },
]} />

### Mistake 12 — Omitting do/does in negative sentences

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "She not understand the new system.", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "She does not understand the new system.", note: "English negatives require do/does + not. The negative cannot attach directly to the main verb." },
]} />

## Section J — Pronunciation Guide

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Ending Type", "Pronunciation", "Example Verbs", "IPA"]}
  rows={[
    ["After voiceless consonants (p, t, k, f)", "/s/", "stops, works, laughs", "/stɒps/, /wɜːks/, /lɑːfs/"],
    ["After voiced consonants and vowels", "/z/", "reads, calls, agrees, runs", "/riːdz/, /kɔːlz/, /rʌnz/"],
    ["After sibilants (-s, -sh, -ch, -x)", "/ɪz/", "passes, watches, fixes", "/pɑːsɪz/, /wɒtʃɪz/, /fɪksɪz/"],
    ["Irregular: goes", "/z/ sound", "goes", "/gəʊz/"],
    ["Irregular: does", "/dʌz/", "does", "/dʌz/ — NOT /duːz/"],
    ["Irregular: says", "/sɛz/", "says", "/sɛz/ — NOT /seɪz/"],
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="tip" title="The /ɪz/ Rule">

The *-es* form (*watches, passes, teaches*) adds an extra syllable. Native speakers naturally produce this extra syllable; learners often swallow it, producing *watch* instead of *watch-iz*.

</Callout>
