<Callout type="abstract" title="Unit Overview">

The verb *be* is the most frequently used word in English — and also the most irregular. Whether it sits alone as a main verb or silently powers a progressive tense or passive structure, mastering *be* across all tenses and functions separates an intermediate learner from a confident, accurate speaker. This unit takes you from a solid revision of the basics to nuanced structures you may have been skipping for years.

**By the end of this unit you will:**

- Command all forms of *be* across present, past, future and perfect structures
- Distinguish *be* as a main verb, linking verb, and auxiliary verb
- Understand contracted vs full forms and when to use each
- Avoid the 12 most common mistakes with *be* at intermediate/upper-intermediate level
- Use *be* confidently in progressive, passive, and emphatic structures

</Callout>

<Callout type="quote">

*"To be, or not to be — that is the question."*

— William Shakespeare, *Hamlet*

</Callout>

## Section A — The Three Lives of Be

Before drilling conjugations, you need to understand why *be* behaves the way it does. Unlike most English verbs, *be* can wear three completely different hats in the same sentence.

### A1 — Be as a Main / Linking Verb

When *be* is the main verb, it links a subject to a description, identity, or location. Think of it as an **equals sign**.

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Function", "Example", "What BE connects"]}
  rows={[
    ["Identity", "She is a software engineer.", "Subject → Noun (who they are)"],
    ["Quality / Adjective", "The presentation was impressive.", "Subject → Adjective (what they are like)"],
    ["Location", "Your keys are in the drawer.", "Subject → Place (where they are)"],
    ["Age", "He was only nineteen when he wrote that.", "Subject → Age"],
    ["Measurement", "The bridge is 400 metres long.", "Subject → Measurement"],
    ["Price / Value", "These seats are £60 each.", "Subject → Price"],
    ["Existence (there + be)", "There are three unread messages.", "Introduces existence of something"],
  ]}
/>

### A2 — Be as an Auxiliary Verb (Helper)

Here *be* does not carry the main meaning — it serves another verb. You cannot drop it without breaking the sentence.

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Grammatical Purpose", "Structure", "Example"]}
  rows={[
    ["Progressive tenses", "be + -ing", "They are reviewing the contract right now."],
    ["Passive voice", "be + past participle", "The report was submitted on time."],
    ["Future (going to)", "be + going to + infinitive", "We are going to launch in March."],
    ["Passive progressive", "be + being + past participle", "The bridge is being repaired."],
  ]}
/>

## Section B — The Complete Conjugation of Be

English has only one verb that changes this dramatically across tenses. Study these tables not just to memorise, but to notice the patterns.

### B1 — Present Simple

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Subject", "Positive", "Negative", "Contracted Neg.", "Question"]}
  rows={[
    ["I", "am", "am not", "I'm not (NOT amn't)", "Am I …?"],
    ["You", "are", "are not", "aren't", "Are you …?"],
    ["He / She / It", "is", "is not", "isn't", "Is he/she/it …?"],
    ["We", "are", "are not", "aren't", "Are we …?"],
    ["They", "are", "are not", "aren't", "Are they …?"],
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="warning" title="Special Note on amn't">

In standard British and American English, **"amn't" does not exist.** The only contracted negative of "I am" is **"I'm not."**

In speech, informal questions use *"Aren't I?"* (even though this is technically illogical). In Scottish and Irish English, *"amn't"* is occasionally used — but avoid it in formal writing or exams.

</Callout>

## Section C — Contractions: When, Why & How

Contractions are not laziness — they are the **natural rhythm of English**. Using full forms where a native speaker would use contractions sounds unnatural and even cold. However, knowing when NOT to contract is equally important.

## Section D — Building Questions with Be

Forming questions with *be* is structurally simpler than with most other verbs because **be does NOT use do/does/did.** The rule is direct: move *be* before the subject.

<Callout type="note" title="The Core Rule">

- **STATEMENT:** Subject + be + …
- **QUESTION:** Be + Subject + … ?

</Callout>

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Tense", "Statement", "Question"]}
  rows={[
    ["Present", "The project is ready.", "Is the project ready?"],
    ["Present", "You are the team leader.", "Are you the team leader?"],
    ["Past", "The meeting was productive.", "Was the meeting productive?"],
    ["Past", "They were informed in advance.", "Were they informed in advance?"],
    ["Future", "The results will be published.", "Will the results be published?"],
    ["Pres. Perfect", "She has been promoted.", "Has she been promoted?"],
    ["Past Perfect", "The data had been corrupted.", "Had the data been corrupted?"],
  ]}
/>

## Section E — There + Be: Existence and Introduction

One of the most common intermediate-level mistakes is confusing *there is/are* with *it is.* These structures have completely different jobs.

<Callout type="note" title="Key Distinction">

- **THERE + BE** = introduces **existence** of something new to the listener
- **IT + BE** = refers to something **already known**, or describes time/weather/distance

</Callout>

<GrammarTable
  headers={["There + be: introducing new information", "It + be: describing / referring back"]}
  rows={[
    ["There is a problem with your application.", "It is a serious problem. (= the problem just mentioned)"],
    ["There are 47 students in this class.", "It is the largest class in the school."],
    ["There was a long silence after the announcement.", "It was an uncomfortable silence."],
    ["Is there a pharmacy near here?", "It's just around the corner. (= distance/location)"],
    ["There will be three rounds of interviews.", "It will be a challenging process."],
  ]}
/>

### E1 — Agreement: there is vs there are

The verb agrees with the **noun that follows**, not with "there". This is where intermediate learners make the most errors.

<ExampleBlock
  examples={[
    { sentence: "There is many options available.", wrong: true, note: "options is plural — use are" },
    { sentence: "There are many options available.", note: "Correct" },
    { sentence: "There are an interesting documentary on tonight.", wrong: true, note: "a documentary is singular — use is" },
    { sentence: "There is an interesting documentary on tonight.", note: "Correct" },
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="tip" title="Informal Spoken Exception">

In fast informal speech, *"there's"* is often used with plural nouns by native speakers.

- *"There's a few things I want to discuss."* (spoken, informal — acceptable)
- *"There are a few things I want to discuss."* (always correct — use this in writing)

</Callout>

## Section F — Be in Progressive Structures

When *be* acts as an auxiliary in progressive tenses, a crucial distinction emerges that many intermediate learners miss: the difference between a **state** and **behaviour**.

### F1 — States vs Behaviour: The Key Distinction

Stative adjectives describe permanent or temporary states. When *be* is used in the progressive, it signals **current behaviour** — something the person is actively doing.

<Callout type="example" title="Compare these two sentences">

**"She is being very patient."** → She is *actively behaving* patiently — it is something she is *doing* right now.

**"She is patient."** → This is a *permanent quality* of her character.

</Callout>

<GrammarTable
  headers={["State (simple be = character)", "Behaviour (progressive be = current action)"]}
  rows={[
    ["He is very rude.", "He is being very rude to the customer. ⚠ Call the manager."],
    ["She's usually careful.", "She's being unusually careless with the data today."],
    ["The children are quite well-behaved.", "The children are being absolutely impossible this morning."],
    ["You're selfish.", "You're being selfish by not sharing the credit."],
    ["I'm not silly.", "I wasn't being silly — I was making a serious suggestion."],
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="important" title="Adjectives that CANNOT be used with progressive be">

Feelings as states: *depressed, tired, excited, worried, happy, sad*

- ❌ *"She is being depressed."* → ✅ *"She is depressed."*
- ❌ *"I'm being tired."* → ✅ *"I'm tired."*

</Callout>

These are the errors that intermediate and upper-intermediate learners make most frequently.

### Mistake 1 — Using do/does/did with be

  { sentence: "She isn't at home. / She's not at home.", note: "NEVER use do/does/did to negate be" },

### Mistake 2 — Adding be after the question form

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "Are they be students?", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "Are they students?", note: "NEVER add be after the question form of be" },
]} />

### Mistake 3 — Number agreement with there

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "There is many problems.", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "There are many problems.", note: "be agrees with the noun after it, not with there" },

### Mistake 4 — Be + agree

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "I am agree with you.", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "I agree with you.", note: "agree is a full verb, not an adjective — never use be + agree" },

### Mistake 5 — Boring vs Bored

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "He is very boring in class.", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "He is very bored in class.", note: "-ed = how a person feels; -ing = what causes the feeling" },
]} />

### Mistake 6 — I am boring of

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "I am boring of this routine.", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "I am bored of / with this routine.", note: "Use bored, not boring, for personal feelings" },
]} />

### Mistake 7 — The news are

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "The news are shocking.", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "The news is shocking.", note: "News is always singular. Same rule: information, advice" },
]} />

### Mistake 8 — Being tired

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "She was being very tired yesterday.", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "She was very tired yesterday.", note: "States and feelings cannot be progressive" },
]} />

### Mistake 9 — Amn't

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "I amn't sure about that.", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "I'm not sure about that. / I am not sure.", note: "amn't does not exist in standard English" },

### Mistake 10 — Was you

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "Where was you yesterday?", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "Where were you yesterday?", note: "You always takes were, never was" },

### Mistake 11 — Is there a problems

## Section H — Pronunciation Guide

The way *be* sounds in natural speech is very different from how it looks in writing. Weak forms, linking sounds and contraction rhythms are essential for natural spoken English.

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Word / Phrase", "Pronunciation", "Tip"]}
  rows={[
    ["am", "/æm/ (strong) → /əm/ (weak)", "In natural speech: I'm /aɪm/ — 'What am I doing?' sounds like 'What'm I doing?'"],
    ["are", "/ɑː/ (strong) → /ə/ (weak)", "'You are' in flow sounds like 'yor' — are reduces to /ə/. Don't over-stress it."],
    ["is", "/ɪz/ (strong) → /z/ or /s/ (weak)", "After voiced sounds: 'he's' = /hiːz/. After voiceless: 'it's' = /ɪts/"],
    ["was", "/wɒz/ (strong) → /wəz/ (weak)", "'He was there' → /hiː wəz ðeə/ — most learners over-stress was."],
    ["were", "/wɜː/ (strong) → /wə/ (weak)", "'They were' → /ðeɪwə/ in fast speech. Strong form used for contrast: 'Yes, we WERE.'"],
    ["isn't", "/ɪzənt/", "Do NOT pronounce the t in natural speech: /ɪzən/. The t is glottalized."],
    ["wasn't", "/wɒzənt/", "Common error: 'woz-ant.' Correct: /wɒzənt/ with glottal stop."],
    ["weren't", "/wɜːnt/", "Rhymes with 'burnt.' Many learners say 'we-rent' — incorrect."],
    ["there's", "/ðeəz/", "Sounds like 'thers.' Do not pause between 'there' and 's'."],
    ["there are", "/ðeər ɑː/", "In fast speech: 'therer' — the /r/ links to 'are'. Pausing sounds unnatural."],
    ["being", "/biːɪŋ/", "Two syllables: BEE-ing. Many learners say 'bean' (one syllable). Keep both clear."],
    ["been", "/biːn/ (British) → /bɪn/ (American)", "British: rhymes with 'seen.' American: rhymes with 'bin.' Be consistent."],
  ]}
/>
