<Callout type="abstract" title="Unit Overview">

The structure *there + be* is one of the most essential tools in English. It allows speakers and writers to introduce new information naturally, signal what exists in a place or time, and frame the world before describing it in detail. Without it, English sentences become awkward and unnatural. Mastering this structure — including its agreement rules, its contrast with *it is*, and its advanced impersonal forms — lifts writing from mechanical to genuinely fluent.

**By the end of this unit you will be able to:**

- Use *there is / there are* correctly with singular and plural subjects in all major tenses
- Avoid the most common agreement errors involving collective nouns, indefinite subjects, and informal speech
- Distinguish clearly between *there is* (introducing new information) and *it is* (referring to known information)
- Use advanced impersonal constructions such as *there seems to be*, *there tends to be*, and *there appears to be* in formal and academic writing
- Recognise and produce both formal and informal register variations of *there + be* structures

</Callout>

## Section A — The Core Structure: What *there is* Actually Does

The phrase *there is* (and all its forms) is used to **introduce** the existence of something — to tell the listener or reader that something exists, or is present, at a particular place or time. This is different from describing something we already know about.

Think of *there is* as a spotlight: it shines a beam onto a new object and says *"Look — this exists."* Once the audience knows what you're talking about, you switch to *it* or *the* to discuss it further.

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Function", "Example", "Notes"]}
  rows={[
    ["Introduce existence", "There is a crack in the wall.", "New information — listener doesn't know yet"],
    ["Indicate presence", "There are three candidates.", "Points to the group as a fact"],
    ["Deny existence", "There isn't much time.", "Negative existence"],
    ["Question existence", "Is there a pharmacy nearby?", "Asking if something exists"],
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="note" title="Key Insight">

*There* in *there is* is **NOT** a place adverb. It has no meaning by itself — it is a grammatical subject placeholder. The real subject comes after the verb: *There is **a problem**.* The problem is the subject; *there* is a dummy word holding the grammatical position.

</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't Confuse These Two Theres">

- *"Is **there** a bank near here?"* — grammatical placeholder (*there is*)
- *"The bank is over **there**."* — place adverb (location)

In speech, the place adverb *there* is stressed. The grammatical *there* is usually unstressed and often contracted: *there's*.

</Callout>

## Section C — Agreement: The Essential Rule

The verb in a *there + be* construction agrees with the **real subject** — the noun that comes **after** the verb, not with *there*.

<Callout type="note" title="The Agreement Formula: there + verb + real subject">

The verb matches the real subject in number.

</Callout>

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Real Subject", "Correct Form", "Incorrect Form"]}
  rows={[
    ["a solution (singular)", "There is a solution.", "There are a solution. ✗"],
    ["several issues (plural)", "There are several issues.", "There is several issues. ✗"],
    ["no parking (uncountable)", "There is no parking here.", "There are no parking. ✗"],
    ["a lot of people", "There are a lot of people.", "There is a lot of people. ✗"],
    ["a number of errors", "There are a number of errors.", "There is a number of errors. ✗"],
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="warning" title="The Tricky Cases">

- **Uncountable nouns always take singular:** *There is traffic. / There is violence.*
- **"A lot of / plenty of" take plural when countable:** *There are a lot of questions.*
- **"A number of" is always plural:** *There are a number of reasons.*
- **"The number of" is always singular:** *The number of applicants **is** rising.* (This does not use *there*, but confusion with *there are a number of* is common.)

</Callout>

  { sentence: "There is many advantages to this approach.", wrong: true },

]} />

### Mistake 2 — Using *it is* to introduce new information

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "A new policy. It is effective in reducing costs.", wrong: true, note: "introduces a topic for the first time — it is assumes the listener already knows which policy" },
  { sentence: "There is a new policy. It is effective in reducing costs.", note: "there is introduces something new; it is refers back to something already mentioned" },
]} />

### Mistake 3 — Contracting before a plural in formal writing

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "There's over forty families affected by this.", wrong: true, note: "formal context" },
  { sentence: "There are over forty families affected by this.", note: "in formal or written English, there's before a plural is non-standard" },
]} />

### Mistake 4 — Using *have* instead of *there is*

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "In this city have a lot of problems with public transport.", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "In this city, there are a lot of problems with public transport.", note: "English uses there + be to introduce existence. Using have here is a direct translation error from languages that use have for this purpose." },
]} />

### Mistake 5 — Omitting *there* entirely

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "Is a problem with the heating.", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "There is a problem with the heating.", note: "English requires the dummy subject there — the verb cannot begin a statement without a subject" },
]} />

### Mistake 6 — Wrong tense of *be*

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "There is a major strike last year.", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "There was a major strike last year.", note: "last year signals past time — use was / were" },
]} />

### Mistake 7 — Using *there is* with a definite subject

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "There is the solution we need.", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "The solution we need is a change in policy.", note: "there is introduces indefinite, new information. Definite subjects (with the) are already known — don't use this structure for them." },
]} />

### Mistake 8 — Wrong form in the perfect

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "There has been many accidents on this road.", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "There have been many accidents on this road.", note: "accidents is plural — use have been" },
]} />

### Mistake 9 — *There seems* without *to be*

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "There seems a problem with the application.", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "There seems to be a problem with the application.", note: "seem (and appear, tend) must be followed by to be in this construction" },
]} />

### Mistake 10 — Using *there is* for actions

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "There was arrived a new manager.", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "A new manager arrived.", note: "there + be is for existence, not for actions. Use a normal sentence with the subject in the usual position." },
]} />

### Mistake 11 — Forgetting agreement in questions

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "Is there any good restaurants near here?", wrong: true },
  { sentence: "Are there any good restaurants near here?", note: "restaurants is plural — the question form must be are there" },
]} />

### Mistake 12 — Using *there* with a known, specific subject

<ExampleBlock examples={[
  { sentence: "There is my laptop on the desk.", wrong: true },

## Section D — *There is* vs *It is*: The Critical Distinction

This is one of the most important — and most misunderstood — distinctions in English. Both structures use a form of *be*, but they do fundamentally different things.

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Feature", "there is", "it is"]}
  rows={[
    ["Function", "Introduces new, indefinite information", "Refers to something already known"],
    ["Subject type", "Indefinite (a, some, any, no)", "Definite (the, this, that, pronoun)"],
    ["Discourse position", "Appears first when introducing a topic", "Appears after the topic is established"],
    ["Example", "There is a report on your desk.", "It is the Haines report — from last month."],
  ]}
/>

**A practical test:** If you could replace the noun with *the thing we've just been talking about* and the sentence still makes sense, use *it is*. If the noun is brand new to the conversation, use *there is*.

<Callout type="example" title="Context Demonstration — A colleague calls you over">

1. *"There's **a message** for you at reception."* ← NEW information; listener doesn't know yet
2. *"It's **from** the Tokyo office."* ← Referring back to the message we just introduced
3. *"It says there will be **a delay** in the shipment."* ← New detail inside the message
4. *"The delay is **expected** to last two weeks."* ← Now we've moved to describing what we know

</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Common Register Trap">

Learners often use *it is* to introduce new things because they translate from their native language. In many languages, the equivalent of *"it is a problem"* works for new information. In English, it sounds as if you and the listener already know which problem you mean. Always ask: *is this the first time this information is entering the conversation?*

</Callout>

## Section E — Advanced Impersonal Constructions

At B2 level and above, particularly in academic writing, journalism, and formal speech, *there + be* combines with other verbs and structures to create more nuanced, cautious, or sophisticated statements.

### With *seem* / *appear*

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Structure", "Example", "Register"]}
  rows={[
    ["There seems to be", "There seems to be a fundamental flaw in the plan.", "Formal / Academic"],
    ["There appears to be", "There appears to be some confusion about the deadline.", "Formal / Business"],
    ["There seemed to be", "There seemed to be no easy solution.", "Formal narrative"],
  ]}
/>

### With Modal Verbs

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Structure", "Meaning / Use", "Example"]}
  rows={[
    ["There may be", "Possibility (neutral)", "There may be additional costs involved."],
    ["There might be", "Possibility (more tentative)", "There might be a way around this."],
    ["There must be", "Logical certainty", "There must be a simpler explanation."],
    ["There can't be", "Logical impossibility", "There can't be two people with that name."],
    ["There could be", "Hypothetical possibility", "There could be serious consequences."],
    ["There should be", "Expectation / obligation", "There should be a formal review process."],
  ]}
/>

### With Adjectives: *certain / likely / sure / bound*

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Structure", "Example"]}
  rows={[
    ["There is certain to be", "There is certain to be an inquiry after this incident."],
    ["There is likely to be", "There is likely to be some resistance from the board."],
    ["There is bound to be", "There is bound to be some confusion at first."],
    ["There is sure to be", "There is sure to be a better offer on the market."],
  ]}
/>

### With *need / point / use / sense*

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Structure", "Example"]}
  rows={[
    ["There is no need to", "There is no need to panic."],
    ["There is no point in", "There is no point in arguing about it now."],
    ["There is no use in", "There is no use in waiting any longer."],
    ["There is little sense in", "There is little sense in delaying the decision further."],
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="tip" title="Academic Writing Tip">

In academic writing, *there seems to be* and *there appears to be* are preferred over the blunt *there is* when you are drawing a conclusion from evidence rather than stating a fact. This is called **epistemic hedging** — showing you are interpreting, not declaring.

Compare:

- *There is a link between pollution and respiratory illness.* (states as fact)
- *There appears to be a link between pollution and respiratory illness.* (based on evidence; appropriately cautious)

</Callout>

## Section F — Formal vs Informal Register

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Feature", "Informal", "Formal / Written"]}
  rows={[
    ["Contraction before plural", "There's a lot of problems.", "There are a number of problems."],
    ["There's before plural", "Widely used in speech", "Avoided in formal writing"],
    ["Modal combinations", "There might be something wrong.", "There appears to be a discrepancy."],
    ["Hedging language", "Maybe there's a reason.", "There may well be an underlying cause."],
    ["Negative", "There isn't any point.", "There is little point in pursuing this course of action."],
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="example" title="Same Idea, Two Registers">

**Informal (spoken / WhatsApp):** *"Hey — there's loads of traffic on the ring road. There might be an accident or something."*

**Formal (news report):** *"There are significant delays on the ring road this evening. There appears to be a collision near Junction 7, and there is likely to be further disruption until the road is cleared."*

</Callout>

## Section G — Pronunciation Guide

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Form", "IPA", "Tip"]}
  rows={[
    ["there is", "/ðɛr ɪz/", "Each word pronounced clearly — used in formal/careful speech"],
    ["there's", "/ðɛrz/", "Standard contraction — very common in speech"],
    ["there are", "/ðɛr ɑː/", "Second word often reduced: /ðərə/ in fast speech"],
    ["there was", "/ðɛr wɒz/", "was reduced to /wəz/ in natural speech"],
    ["there were", "/ðɛr wɜː/", "were often reduced: /ðə wə/ in fast speech"],
    ["there will be", "/ðɛr wɪl biː/", "Often contracted to there'll be /ðɛrl biː/ in speech"],
    ["is there", "/ɪz ðɛr/", "In questions — is is stressed, there unstressed"],
    ["aren't there", "/ɑːnt ðɛr/", "Question tag — aren't is stressed"],
    ["there (place adverb)", "/ðɛr/ (stressed)", "Stressed — contrasts with unstressed grammatical there"],
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="tip">

In fast natural speech, *"there's a"* sounds almost like *"thezza"* and *"there are some"* sounds like *"theresome."* Train your ear to catch this reduction — it appears constantly in podcasts, films, and real conversations.

</Callout>
