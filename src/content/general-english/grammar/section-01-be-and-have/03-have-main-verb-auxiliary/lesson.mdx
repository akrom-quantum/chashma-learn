<Callout type="abstract" title="Unit Overview">

The verb *have* is one of the most overworked words in English. It carries three distinct identities at once: it expresses what you own or experience, it launches fixed action phrases like *have a shower*, and it holds entire tenses together as a grammatical auxiliary. Mixing these roles — or not knowing when *have got* can step in and when it absolutely cannot — is one of the most persistent errors at B1–B2 level. This unit gives you a complete map of how *have* works across all three roles and both major varieties of English.

**By the end of this unit you will be able to:**

- Use *have* correctly as a main verb expressing possession, relationships, characteristics, and states
- Use *have got* as a natural British English alternative and know exactly where it cannot be used
- Produce *have* action expressions (*have a shower, have a look*) confidently, without confusing them with possession uses
- Distinguish *have* as a perfect auxiliary from *have* as a main verb — including in sentences where both appear
- Handle the formal vs informal register differences across British and American English

</Callout>

<Callout type="quote">

*"You can have it all, but you can't do it all."*

— Michelle Pfeiffer

</Callout>

## Section A — The Three Lives of *have*

Before anything else, you need to see the big picture. *Have* operates in three completely different grammatical roles. Each role has its own rules, its own restrictions, and its own feel.

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Role", "What It Does", "Example", "Got possible?"]}
  rows={[
    ["Main verb — possession/state", "Expresses ownership, relationships, characteristics, illnesses", "She has a headache.", "✔ She's got a headache."],
    ["Main verb — action", "Fixed expressions for experiences and activities", "I'm going to have a shower.", "✗ I'm going to have got a shower."],
    ["Auxiliary verb", "Forms perfect tenses with a past participle", "I have finished the report.", "✗ I've got finished the report."],
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="note" title="The Single Most Important Rule">

**Got** can ONLY substitute for *have* in the possession/state role. It cannot enter action expressions or auxiliary uses — ever. This one rule eliminates the majority of errors with *have got*.

</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Do NOT use had got in the past">

- *She **had** a bad cold last week.* ✔
- *She had got a bad cold last week.* ✗

*Got*-forms are most natural in the present. In the past, use plain *had*.

</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Do NOT use have got in the future">

- *I **will have** a new job soon.* ✔
- *I will have got a new job soon.* ✗

*Got* is not used with *will* to express future possession.

</Callout>

## Section C — *have got*: The British Alternative

In modern British English, *have got* is the most natural way to express possession, relationships, and states in the present. It means exactly the same as *have/has* — the *got* adds nothing to the meaning, but it makes the sentence feel more natural and conversational.

<Callout type="note" title="Got Adds Nothing to Meaning">

- *I have a problem.* = *I've got a problem.*
- *She has a sister.* = *She's got a sister.*

The meaning is identical. The difference is register and regional preference.

</Callout>

### Full Form Table — *have got*

<GrammarTable
  headers={[" ", "Positive", "Negative", "Question"]}
  rows={[
    ["I / you / we / they", "I've got a meeting.", "I haven't got a meeting.", "Have I got a meeting?"],
    ["he / she / it", "She's got a car.", "She hasn't got a car.", "Has she got a car?"],
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="tip" title="Key Pattern for Questions and Negatives with have got">

With *have got*, questions are formed by inverting *have/has* and the subject — **no** *do/does*.

- *Have you got a pen?* (NOT *Do you have got a pen?*)
- *Has she got any brothers?* (NOT *Does she has got any brothers?*)

This is the opposite of regular *have* questions which use *do/does*.

</Callout>

### *have got* vs *do/does have* — When to Use Which

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Situation", "Preferred Form", "Example"]}
  rows={[
    ["Informal spoken British English", "have got", "Have you got the time?"],
    ["Formal written British English", "have / do have", "Do you have adequate support?"],
    ["American English (all registers)", "have / do have", "Do you have a minute?"],
    ["Short answers", "have/has/haven't/hasn't", "'Have you got a pen?' 'Yes, I have.'"],
    ["Question tags", "have/has/haven't/hasn't", "You've got a car, haven't you?"],
    ["Habitual/repeated actions", "have only", "I often have headaches."],

/>

<Callout type="warning" title="Got-Forms and Habitual Actions">

When *have* expresses something that happens regularly — a habit or repeated event — *got* is NOT used.

- *We **have** meetings every Monday.* ✔ (habit)
- *We've got meetings every Monday.* ✗
- *Do you often **have** colds?* ✔ (habit)
- *Have you often got colds?* ✗

</Callout>

## Section D — *have* for Actions: Fixed Expressions

This is the role that confuses learners the most — and where *have got* is completely impossible.

When *have* appears in fixed expressions describing activities, experiences, and events, it behaves like a completely ordinary verb. It can appear in all tenses, take *do/does/did* for questions and negatives, and take progressive forms.

### Common *have* Action Expressions

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Category", "Expressions"]}
  rows={[
    ["Meals and drinks", "have breakfast / lunch / dinner / a coffee / a drink / something to eat"],
    ["Washing and grooming", "have a shower / a bath / a wash / a shave"],
    ["Rest and leisure", "have a rest / a sleep / a lie-down / a dream / a holiday / a day off"],
    ["Social events", "have a party / a meeting / a conversation / a chat / a talk"],
    ["Travel and experiences", "have a good flight / trip / journey / a good time / a bad day"],
    ["Physical experiences", "have a baby / an accident / an operation / a nervous breakdown"],
    ["Attempts", "have a try / a go / a look / a word with someone"],
    ["Problems", "have difficulty / trouble / a problem with something"],
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="note" title="Why have got Is Impossible Here">

*Have a shower* means "perform the activity of showering." The noun (*a shower*) describes an action, not a possession. You cannot possess a shower in this sense — you perform it.

*Got* only substitutes when the noun after *have* is something you own, carry, or experience as a state. A shower, a look, and a rest are none of these things.

</Callout>

### Grammar of Action *have*

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Feature", "Action have", "Possessive have"]}
  rows={[
    ["Progressive possible?", "✔  She's having a shower.", "✗  She's having a car."],
    ["do/does/did in questions", "✔  Did you have a good time?", "✔  Do you have a car?"],
    ["have got substitution", "✗  impossible", "✔  in British English"],
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="example" title="Seeing the Difference in Context">

- *She **has** a bad back.* — possession/state → progressive impossible; *got* possible
- *She **is having** a bad day.* — action expression → progressive natural; *got* impossible

- *He **has** a bicycle.* — possession → *He's got a bicycle.*
- *He **is having** a rest.* — action → cannot say *He's having got a rest.*

</Callout>

## Section E — *have* as Perfect Auxiliary

When *have* forms perfect tenses (*have done, have seen, have been*, etc.), it is no longer a main verb — it is a grammatical tool. In this role, *have* does not express possession or action. It simply tells you that the event in question has a connection to the present, or that one past event preceded another.

<Callout type="note" title="How to Recognise Auxiliary have">

Auxiliary *have* is always followed by a **past participle** (the third form of a verb).

- *I **have finished** the report.* — *finished* = past participle
- *She **had seen** him before.* — *seen* = past participle
- *They **will have left** by then.* — *left* = past participle

</Callout>

### Perfect Tenses at a Glance

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Tense", "Structure", "Example"]}
  rows={[
    ["Present Perfect", "have/has + past participle", "I have visited three clinics."],
    ["Past Perfect", "had + past participle", "She had never seen such a long queue."],
    ["Future Perfect", "will have + past participle", "By June, they will have treated 500 patients."],
    ["Present Perfect Progressive", "have/has + been + -ing", "He has been waiting for two hours."],
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="warning" title="The Double have Sentence">

Sometimes *have* appears twice in one sentence — once as auxiliary and once as main verb. This confuses many learners who assume they've made an error.

- *Have you **had** an invitation?* → First *have* = auxiliary | *had* = main verb (possession/receipt)
- *I **have had** a terrible headache all day.* → *have* = auxiliary | *had* = main verb (experience/state)
- *We **haven't had** a holiday in three years.* → *haven't* = auxiliary negative | *had* = main verb (action expression)

These double *have* sentences are completely correct and very common in English.

</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Got Cannot Replace Auxiliary have">

- *Have you seen this?* ✔
- *Have you got seen this?* ✗ — *got* cannot enter the auxiliary role.

The only test you need: if *have* is followed by a past participle, it is auxiliary. *Got* is impossible.

</Callout>

<Callout type="note" title="The Safe Middle Ground">

If you are unsure which variety is expected — in an exam, a professional email, or an international context — use *do/does/don't/doesn't have*. This form is grammatically correct, natural, and accepted in both British and American English at all levels of formality.

</Callout>

<Callout type="example" title="Same Situation — Two Registers">

**Informal (phone call to a friend):** *"Have you got a sec? I've got a bit of a problem — I've been having terrible headaches and I haven't had a proper night's sleep in weeks."*

**Formal (letter to a doctor):** *"I am writing to request an appointment. I have been experiencing persistent headaches and have not had adequate sleep for several weeks. I have not had a consultation since last year."*

</Callout>

## Section I — Pronunciation Guide

<GrammarTable
  headers={["Form", "IPA", "Tip"]}
  rows={[
    ["have (full)", "/hæv/", "Used when have is stressed or stands alone"],
    ["have (reduced, aux)", "/həv/ or /əv/", "Very reduced in fast speech: 'I've seen' → /aɪv siːn/"],
    ["has (full)", "/hæz/", "Stressed position or emphatic"],
    ["has (reduced)", "/həz/ or /əz/", "'She's got' → /ʃɪz ɡɒt/"],
    ["had (full)", "/hæd/", "Emphatic or sentence-initial"],
    ["had (reduced)", "/həd/ or /əd/", "'I'd seen' → /aɪd siːn/"],
    ["have got", "/həv ɡɒt/", "'ve got is standard: /v ɡɒt/"],
    ["haven't", "/ˈhævənt/", "Final t often dropped in fast speech"],
    ["hasn't", "/ˈhæzənt/", "Don't drop the -s-: not /ˈhænt/"],
    ["had (past aux)", "/həd/", "In 'He'd never seen it' — very reduced"],
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="tip" title="Listening for Auxiliary vs Main Verb">

In natural speech, auxiliary *have* is almost always contracted or heavily reduced. *"I've finished"* sounds like */aɪv ˈfɪnɪʃt/*. Main verb *have* (possession) is often similarly reduced, but is followed by a noun, not a past participle. Train your ear to listen for what comes **after** *have* — a noun (main verb) or a past participle (auxiliary).

</Callout>
