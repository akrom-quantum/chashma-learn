# What Sleep Is For

## Passage

Every animal studied in sufficient detail has been found to sleep. Insects, fish, reptiles, birds, mammals — all show patterns of reduced activity and altered neural states that meet reasonable definitions of sleep. This universality is remarkable. An animal asleep is vulnerable: it cannot forage, defend territory, or escape predators. For sleep to have survived the pressures of evolution across every major animal lineage, its benefits must substantially outweigh these obvious costs. What those benefits actually are has, until recently, remained surprisingly unclear.

The most intuitive hypothesis — that sleep is simply a period of rest — does not survive close inspection. An animal lying quietly but awake uses only slightly less energy than one in deep sleep. Energy conservation cannot therefore justify the elaborate neural state that sleep entails, with its distinct stages, characteristic brainwave patterns, and loss of conscious awareness. Something more is happening that cannot be achieved by simple rest.

A cluster of findings from the past two decades has begun to clarify what that something is. The brain, it turns out, uses sleep to consolidate memories formed during the preceding day. In both animal and human studies, the neural patterns associated with recently learned tasks are replayed during sleep, apparently transferring information from short-term storage in the hippocampus to longer-term representations distributed across the cortex. Subjects deprived of sleep after learning a task perform worse when tested the following day, even when they are given caffeine or other stimulants to keep them alert. The effect appears across a wide range of learning types, from motor skills to factual recall to emotional associations.

A second, more surprising function concerns the physical cleaning of the brain. The brain is unique among organs in that it has no lymphatic vessels to remove waste products. Researchers at the University of Rochester, working with mice in the early 2010s, identified a system — which they called the glymphatic system — that partially fulfils this function. The system becomes markedly more active during sleep. Cerebrospinal fluid flows through channels around blood vessels, carrying away proteins and metabolic by-products that accumulate during waking activity. Among the substances cleared is beta-amyloid, the protein whose build-up is characteristic of Alzheimer's disease. Disturbed or inadequate sleep appears to reduce this clearance, and epidemiological studies have found correlations between chronic sleep deprivation and later risk of dementia, though establishing direct causation remains difficult.

Research on other possible functions continues to expand. Sleep appears to support immune function, with multiple studies linking shorter sleep to worse response to vaccination and higher susceptibility to common infections. Emotional regulation depends on sleep in ways that laboratory experiments can demonstrate in a single night: subjects kept awake for twenty-four hours show exaggerated negative emotional reactions to standardised stimuli, patterns that normalise after a single recovery night. Growth and tissue repair, particularly in children and adolescents, occur disproportionately during deep sleep, when growth hormone is most actively secreted. Each of these functions might, in isolation, not justify the evolutionary persistence of sleep. Collectively, they make the costs of the vulnerability that sleep imposes look increasingly reasonable.

Contemporary societies, by contrast, treat sleep as a flexible resource that can be traded off against more pressing demands. Surveys in industrialised countries typically find that adults sleep roughly an hour less per night than their counterparts did in the mid-twentieth century. The apparent drivers include artificial lighting that suppresses evening melatonin production, digital screens that extend both working and recreational hours into the night, and cultural attitudes that treat short sleep as a sign of dedication rather than a health risk. The public health implications have begun to receive serious attention, though behavioural change at population level has proven difficult. Most people, asked directly, recognise that they would benefit from more sleep. Relatively few act on this recognition consistently, a gap that sleep researchers have yet to find effective ways of closing.
