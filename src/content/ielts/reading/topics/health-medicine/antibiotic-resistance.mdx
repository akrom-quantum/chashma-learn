# The Antibiotic Era and Its Limits

## Passage

When Alexander Fleming noticed, in 1928, that a stray mould had killed the bacteria growing on one of his laboratory plates, he could not have predicted the scale of the revolution his observation would set in motion. Fleming himself spent little time pursuing the discovery, and it was not until more than a decade later that Howard Florey and Ernst Chain, working in Oxford, developed methods to produce penicillin in useful quantities. Their work arrived during the Second World War, when thousands of soldiers were dying from previously minor wounds that became infected. Penicillin, first mass-produced in 1944, transformed those infections from lethal to routinely treatable. It was the beginning of what physicians would later call the antibiotic era.

The benefits of that era have been extraordinary. Diseases that had routinely killed — bacterial pneumonia, tuberculosis, scarlet fever, sepsis following childbirth — became curable. Surgery, previously constrained by the near-certainty of post-operative infection, could be attempted in places and on conditions that would have been unthinkable before. Average life expectancy in much of the world rose substantially, driven partly by vaccination and partly by the routine availability of effective antibiotic treatment for infections that would previously have proven fatal. By the 1970s, some public health experts spoke confidently of a future in which infectious disease, while not eliminated, had been decisively controlled.

Fleming himself, in his 1945 Nobel Prize lecture, had warned against the complacency that this confidence would later reflect. He noted that bacteria exposed to too little penicillin for too short a time could develop resistance, and he cautioned that careless use of the drug would render it useless over time. The warning was prescient but largely unheeded. Antibiotics became one of the most widely prescribed classes of medication in the world, often for conditions — such as viral infections — against which they have no effect. They were also used heavily in agriculture, where low doses added to animal feed were found to promote growth in livestock, regardless of whether any animal was actually sick. The result of these practices has been exactly what Fleming predicted.

Resistance arises through the ordinary processes of evolution. Within any large population of bacteria, a few individuals by chance possess genetic variations that make them slightly less susceptible to a given antibiotic. Exposed to that antibiotic in insufficient doses or for insufficient duration, the vulnerable bacteria die and the resistant ones survive to reproduce. After enough cycles, the resistant strain dominates. Worse, bacteria can exchange genetic material directly with other bacteria, so that resistance genes acquired by one species can spread horizontally into entirely unrelated species. A resistance trait that appears in bacteria on a pig farm may, through this mechanism, reach hospital pathogens thousands of kilometres away.

The pace of new antibiotic development has not kept up. Between 1940 and 1980, pharmaceutical companies brought dozens of new antibiotic classes to market. In the four decades since, almost no novel class has been successfully developed. The reasons are partly economic — antibiotics are prescribed for short courses and sold at low prices, making them less profitable to develop than drugs for chronic conditions — and partly scientific, as the easiest antibiotic targets have long since been exploited. Several strains of bacteria have now been documented that resist every antibiotic currently available, and the Centers for Disease Control in the United States has classified these cases as an urgent public health threat.

Responses have converged around three strategies. The first is stewardship — restricting the prescription of antibiotics to cases where they are genuinely needed and reducing their use in agriculture. The second is surveillance: tracking resistance patterns globally to anticipate outbreaks and guide treatment. The third is research and development, supported by public funding to compensate for the weak commercial incentives. None of these is likely, on its own, to reverse the trend, but together they may slow it sufficiently to give researchers time to develop alternatives. What is no longer plausible is the assumption, prevalent in the middle of the last century, that infectious disease is a solved problem.
