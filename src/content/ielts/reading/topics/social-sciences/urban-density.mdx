# Why Cities Keep Growing

## Passage

At the start of the nineteenth century, fewer than three per cent of the world's population lived in cities. Today, more than fifty-five per cent do, and the United Nations projects that figure will rise to nearly seventy per cent by the middle of the twenty-first century. This migration to urban centres has persisted across vastly different political systems, economic conditions, and cultural contexts — a degree of consistency that suggests something about cities fundamentally draws people toward them, regardless of circumstance.

Economists have long argued that the pull of cities derives primarily from what they call agglomeration effects. When large numbers of people and firms concentrate in the same geographic area, they become more productive per capita than they would be in isolation. A factory in a city benefits from a deep pool of workers with relevant skills; workers benefit from a wider choice of employers and higher wages. Firms that supply specialised components find enough customers to make their business viable. Knowledge passes informally between workers in overlapping industries, accelerating innovation. These gains compound, which helps explain why cities tend to grow larger over time rather than stabilising at some optimal size.

The same logic extends beyond economics. Cities concentrate cultural activity because artists, performers, and audiences all benefit from proximity to one another. Hospitals specialising in rare conditions cluster in major urban centres because only there can they find enough patients to justify their expertise. Universities draw students from wide catchments and, in turn, attract employers eager to hire their graduates. Each of these patterns reinforces the others, so that a successful city tends to attract further success in self-reinforcing cycles.

Such growth is not without consequence. Housing costs in popular cities have risen sharply over recent decades, pushing long-term residents outward and creating increasingly stratified patterns of settlement in which the wealthiest live near city centres and lower-income workers commute from distant peripheries. Traffic congestion costs major metropolitan areas billions of dollars annually in lost productivity. Air quality in rapidly urbanising cities often deteriorates faster than public health infrastructure can respond. The same concentration of people that creates economic value produces environmental and social strain.

Some planners argue that density itself is not the problem — that poorly designed density is. Compact cities with robust public transport, generous green space, and mixed-use neighbourhoods, where homes, shops, and workplaces coexist, consistently outperform sprawling alternatives on measures of pollution, commuting time, and reported wellbeing. The Dutch city of Utrecht and parts of Copenhagen are frequently cited as evidence that cities can grow in population without becoming more congested or less liveable. The key variable, in these analyses, is how the growth is accommodated rather than whether it is accepted.

A counter-narrative has emerged from the experience of the 2020s, which saw many knowledge workers in wealthy countries relocate to smaller towns when remote work became widespread. Some commentators predicted that this trend would reverse centuries of urban concentration, as digital tools allowed people to access economic opportunities from anywhere. The evidence since has been mixed. Rural and small-town populations in certain regions did rise modestly, but most major cities recovered their populations within a few years. The agglomeration effects that drew people to cities, it seems, proved more durable than the inconveniences that temporarily pushed them away. Whether this balance will hold as work practices continue to evolve remains an open question, but the historical trajectory, for now, has not reversed.

- **durable** (adj) — lasting; not easily destroyed or reversed.
