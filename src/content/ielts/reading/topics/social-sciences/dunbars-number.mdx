# Dunbar's Number and the Limits of Human Community

## Passage

In the early 1990s, British anthropologist Robin Dunbar proposed that there is a natural ceiling on the number of stable social relationships a person can maintain. He arrived at this figure — approximately 150 — not through surveys or observational studies, but through analysis of primate brain sizes. Dunbar had noticed that species of monkeys and apes with larger neocortices tended to live in larger social groups. Extrapolating from this relationship, he calculated what group size the human neocortex would predict. The answer, consistently across several methods of calculation, was around 150.

What made Dunbar's argument persuasive was that the number appeared to match real human patterns. He found it recurring in historical village sizes, in the reported sizes of hunter-gatherer bands, in the membership of small religious congregations, and in the informal limit at which military units tend to lose internal coherence. Roman centuries and early Christian communities clustered near 150. Hutterite farming communities historically split when they exceeded this size because, as members themselves reported, members could no longer enforce behavioural norms through personal relationship alone.

Dunbar's subsequent research refined this number into a hierarchy of concentric circles. Beyond the core 150, he identified an inner layer of about five intimate relationships — typically immediate family and closest friends. Beyond that, around fifteen close friends, then fifty regular social contacts, and finally the full 150 representing everyone with whom a person maintains a genuine personal connection. Further out still lay 500 acquaintances and around 1,500 faces one could recognise, though relationships at those outer levels require minimal cognitive maintenance.

The theory has not been without challenge. Some researchers have argued that the mathematical relationship Dunbar derived from primate brains does not extrapolate cleanly to humans, whose brains differ from other primates in ways not captured by neocortex volume alone. Others have pointed to individual variation — some people clearly maintain substantially larger or smaller networks than the average — and argued that a single number disguises important diversity. Recent studies using large social media datasets have produced mixed results, with some finding effective group sizes close to 150 and others suggesting a substantially wider distribution.

The rise of digital communication has complicated the picture further. Platforms such as Facebook and Twitter allow users to accumulate connections in the thousands, far exceeding any natural limit Dunbar's theory would predict. Yet when researchers examine these networks more closely, they typically find that the vast majority of a user's actual interactions — the comments exchanged, the messages sent, the posts responded to — cluster around a much smaller subset of contacts, often in the range Dunbar's theory suggests. Having thousands of online connections does not appear to translate into meaningfully more relationships; it merely adds names to a list.

Whether or not 150 is the precise number, Dunbar's work has reframed how researchers think about social scale. Organisations, from companies to religious communities, have begun experimenting with structures that respect these apparent cognitive limits — keeping teams small enough that members can know one another directly, or dividing large institutions into smaller units that preserve the feel of a community. The broader implication, that human social life is bounded by biology as much as by culture, continues to attract both interest and debate. What the theory does not claim, Dunbar has been careful to emphasise, is that digital tools make genuine relationships impossible. It suggests only that no technology, however sophisticated, changes the underlying capacity of the human brain for meaningful connection.
