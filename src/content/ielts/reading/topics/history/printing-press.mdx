# Before Gutenberg: The Long Prehistory of Print

## Passage

Johannes Gutenberg is routinely credited with inventing the printing press around 1440 in the German city of Mainz. The invention is said to have sparked a revolution: books became affordable, literacy spread, the Protestant Reformation became possible, and Europe entered a new age of mass communication. While each of these consequences has some historical support, the story as commonly told obscures the fact that Gutenberg's invention was not, strictly speaking, the invention of printing at all. It was the invention of a particular and highly efficient method of printing — one with roots reaching centuries into the past and in regions far from Europe.

The earliest printed texts known to historians were produced in China and Korea hundreds of years before Gutenberg. Woodblock printing — in which a text is carved in reverse onto a single block of wood, inked, and pressed onto paper — was being used in China by the seventh century. The *Diamond Sutra*, a Buddhist scripture printed in 868 and now held by the British Library, is widely regarded as the oldest surviving printed book. Movable type, in which individual characters can be arranged and rearranged to set different pages, was invented in China in the eleventh century by an artisan named Bi Sheng. Korean printers refined movable type further, producing a metal-type edition of a Buddhist text called the *Jikji* in 1377 — more than half a century before Gutenberg.

Why, then, did the European invention produce consequences so much greater than its Asian predecessors? The answer is not that Gutenberg's technical insights were more original, but that they happened to align with conditions that allowed rapid diffusion. European alphabets use a limited set of characters — between twenty and thirty for most languages — whereas Chinese printing required thousands of individual characters, making movable type comparatively unwieldy. European languages were also, in Gutenberg's era, beginning to standardise, so that a single edition of a book could find readers across entire countries. Paper, imported to Europe from the Islamic world and manufactured locally from rags, was cheap and plentiful. A merchant class existed that could fund print shops and generate demand for printed texts.

Gutenberg's specific contributions lay in the details. He developed an oil-based ink that adhered properly to metal type. He designed a mould that allowed type to be cast quickly and uniformly from an alloy of lead, tin, and antimony. He adapted a wine press, already familiar in wine-producing regions, to apply even pressure across a page. None of these individual innovations was especially radical on its own. Taken together, they amounted to an industrial process capable of producing identical copies in quantities that had previously been unimaginable. His 1455 edition of the Bible — produced in around 180 copies — required an investment large enough that Gutenberg himself ended up in litigation with his financial backer and never enjoyed much of the profit.

Within fifty years of that Bible, printing presses had been established in more than 250 European cities. By 1500, an estimated twenty million books had been printed — more than had existed in all the scriptoria of medieval Europe combined. Prices fell, and literacy, though still confined to a minority, began to expand among merchants and artisans. Yet the effects varied considerably by region. In areas where civil or religious authorities exerted tight control over print, the new technology reinforced existing power structures rather than challenging them. Elsewhere, print enabled the rapid circulation of dissenting ideas, most famously during the early Protestant Reformation.

The enduring lesson of this history is that technologies do not produce their social consequences in isolation. The same basic method that enabled European mass communication had existed for centuries elsewhere without producing comparable upheaval. What made the fifteenth-century European experience different was not the invention itself but the conditions — linguistic, economic, political, and religious — into which it arrived.
