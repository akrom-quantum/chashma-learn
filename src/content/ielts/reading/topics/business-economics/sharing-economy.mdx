# The Sharing Economy's Unfinished Promise

## Passage

When the companies now associated with the sharing economy first appeared in the late 2000s, they promised a fundamental change in how economic activity would be organised. Instead of consumers buying goods and services from firms, individuals would rent out their underused assets — a spare room, a car sitting idle most hours of the day, an hour of free time — to other individuals directly. Technology would reduce the transaction costs that had previously made such peer-to-peer exchanges impractical, unlocking value that conventional business models left on the table. Advocates predicted more efficient resource use, new income for ordinary people, and a softer version of capitalism in which communities traded favours rather than companies selling commodities.

The early results appeared to support these predictions. Airbnb, founded in 2008, allowed homeowners to let rooms to travellers at rates often lower than hotels charged and at the same time generate income from property that would otherwise be empty. Uber, launched in 2009, connected passengers with drivers in a matter of minutes, drawing in workers who preferred flexible hours to conventional employment. TaskRabbit, Upwork, and a range of smaller platforms applied similar logic to domestic chores, professional services, and countless other niches. Within a decade, several sharing-economy firms had become among the most valuable private companies in the world, and the practices they pioneered had begun to spread into adjacent industries.

The rhetoric of sharing, however, sat uneasily with the economic reality. The platforms were not cooperatives. They were firms that took substantial fees from each transaction, and their business models depended on attracting enough participants on both sides of the market to make the service useful. The economic rents that had once gone to taxi medallion owners or hotel chains increasingly went to the platforms themselves. Individual drivers and hosts retained a larger share than workers in some older industries, but they also assumed risks — insurance costs, equipment wear, periods of slack demand — that traditional employers had previously absorbed.

The position of workers in these systems has proved particularly contentious. Platforms have generally classified their workers as independent contractors rather than employees, avoiding obligations around minimum wage, overtime, and social insurance. This classification has suited some participants, who value the flexibility of choosing their own hours, but has excluded others from protections they would take for granted in conventional work. Legal challenges to this classification have produced inconsistent outcomes, varying not only between countries but between individual courts and legislatures within the same country. In several jurisdictions, drivers have won reclassification as employees; in others, courts have upheld the independent-contractor model.

The effects on affected industries have been uneven. Incumbent taxi firms in many cities have lost substantial market share, and some cities have witnessed the near-elimination of traditional services. The hotel industry has responded more successfully, with major chains retaining most business travellers while ceding some leisure-market share to short-term rentals. More concerning from a public-policy perspective is the effect of short-term rentals on residential housing. In cities where tourism is concentrated, a substantial share of rental housing has been converted to short-term tourist accommodation, reducing supply for long-term residents and contributing to rising rents. Several cities — Barcelona, Amsterdam, New York — have responded with regulatory restrictions on short-term rentals, and these have begun to stabilise local housing markets with varying success.

What began as a movement associated with small-scale peer exchange has matured into a set of industries with scale and organisational power comparable to the established firms they initially disrupted. Some of the original promises have been kept. Consumers in many cities genuinely enjoy more choice and lower prices than they did fifteen years ago. Workers who could not fit into conventional employment patterns have found ways to earn. Resources that sat idle now generate value. Other promises have proven more complicated. The idea that the sharing economy would distribute economic rewards more widely has been undermined by the dominance of a small number of very large platforms. The long-term trajectory of these arrangements remains uncertain, and the regulatory frameworks still being worked out will shape the answer.

- **sitting idle** (phrase) — not in use.
- **unlocking value** (phrase) — enabling the release of economic benefit.
- **left on the table** (phrase) — available but not taken.
- **commodities** (n) — basic goods that can be traded.
- **let** (v) — to rent out (especially property).
- **niches** (n) — specialised market segments.
- **pioneered** (v) — done or developed first.
- **cooperatives** (n) — businesses owned and run by their members.
- **rents** (n, economic term) — profits earned from control of a scarce resource.
- **slack demand** (phrase) — periods of low customer interest.
- **absorbed** (v) — in economics, taken on as a cost.
- **contentious** (adj) — causing disagreement.
- **incumbent** (adj) — currently holding a position.

- **concentrated** (adj) — gathered in a small area.
- **stabilise** (v) — to make steady.
- **disrupted** (v) — caused major change in.
- **undermined** (v) — weakened; damaged.
