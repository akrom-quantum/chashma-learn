# Why Coral Reefs Are Turning White

## Passage

A healthy coral reef is among the most colourful environments on the planet. Its vibrant appearance comes not from the corals themselves, which are mostly translucent, but from microscopic algae that live within their tissues. The algae, known as zooxanthellae, are photosynthetic. They produce sugars that nourish the coral, and in return the coral provides them with a protected environment and access to sunlight. This relationship is so foundational that without it, most reef-building corals cannot survive.

The partnership, however, is fragile. When water temperatures rise even slightly above the levels corals have adapted to, the relationship breaks down. Stressed corals expel their zooxanthellae, and the brilliant colours disappear almost overnight. What remains is the pale white skeleton beneath the transparent tissue — the phenomenon known as coral bleaching. A bleached coral is not dead, but it is starving. If cooler conditions return within a few weeks, the algae may recolonise the tissue and the coral may survive. If the heat stress persists for longer, or recurs before the coral has recovered, the outcome is usually fatal.

Mass bleaching events were almost unknown before 1980. The first global-scale event was recorded in 1998, when an unusually strong El Niño — a climate pattern that temporarily warms large areas of the Pacific — pushed sea temperatures above their historical averages for months at a time. Reefs in fifty countries experienced significant bleaching. Since then, such events have increased in frequency. A second global event in 2010, a third from 2014 to 2017, and a fourth starting in 2023 have each surpassed their predecessors in both scale and severity. The Great Barrier Reef, previously celebrated as one of the best-protected reef systems in the world, has experienced bleaching severe enough to kill half of its shallow-water corals within a decade.

The underlying cause is not in dispute. The oceans have absorbed more than ninety per cent of the excess heat produced by greenhouse gas emissions over the past several decades. A warming of just one or two degrees Celsius — seemingly modest — pushes tropical seas toward or past the upper limits that reef-building corals have evolved to tolerate. As global temperatures continue to rise, what were once rare extreme heatwaves in the ocean have become frequent enough that many reefs now experience them more often than they can recover between events.

A second environmental pressure compounds the first. Carbon dioxide dissolved in seawater forms carbonic acid, which makes the oceans progressively less alkaline. This acidification weakens corals' ability to build their calcium carbonate skeletons and appears, in laboratory studies, to slow the growth of young corals trying to settle on already-damaged reefs. Unlike warming, which causes visible bleaching, acidification is largely invisible — but over time, its effects may prove no less damaging.

Efforts to protect reefs have taken several forms. Marine protected areas restrict fishing and coastal development, reducing local stressors that compound the effects of warming. Research teams have experimented with breeding corals for heat tolerance, with some trials producing strains capable of surviving temperatures that would bleach conventional corals. More ambitious proposals include artificially shading vulnerable reef sections during predicted heatwaves or transporting heat-adapted corals to endangered reefs elsewhere. None of these interventions can substitute for reducing the underlying cause of warming, but they may buy time for parts of the reef system that would otherwise collapse before climate policies take effect.

The long-term prospects remain uncertain. Some researchers have argued that well-managed reefs in optimal locations may survive in recognisable form through the twenty-first century. Others predict near-total loss of tropical reefs within a few decades if current warming trends continue. What is no longer in dispute is that the reefs humanity has known for the past several thousand years are unlikely to persist unchanged into the next several hundred.
