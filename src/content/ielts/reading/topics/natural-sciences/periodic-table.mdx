# Filling the Gaps in the Periodic Table

## Passage

When Russian chemist Dmitri Mendeleev published his periodic table of elements in 1869, he did something unusual for a scientific publication of the era: he left deliberate gaps. Mendeleev noticed that if the known elements were arranged by atomic weight, their chemical properties recurred in predictable patterns. Yet the pattern broke down in places, with elements appearing to occupy the wrong position. Rather than abandon his system, Mendeleev concluded that the broken pattern indicated missing elements — elements that had not yet been discovered.

This was a striking claim. Mendeleev went further: he used his table to predict not merely that these elements existed, but what their properties would be. The element he called *eka-aluminium*, he asserted, would have a specific atomic weight, a low melting point, and would form a particular kind of oxide. Within fifteen years, all three of these predicted elements — gallium, scandium, and germanium — had been isolated in laboratories, and their measured properties matched Mendeleev's predictions with remarkable accuracy. What had begun as an organisational tool became a predictive instrument of extraordinary power.

The periodic table was not, however, a finished product in 1869. Later discoveries revealed entire categories of elements that Mendeleev's original arrangement could not accommodate. The noble gases — helium, neon, argon, and their relatives — were detected only in the 1890s, when the chemist William Ramsay analysed the residue left after other gases had been chemically removed from air. Because these gases refused to react with anything, they had remained invisible to the chemistry of Mendeleev's generation. Their discovery required not a new element but a new column of the table, inserted on the far right.

A more profound revision came with the understanding that atomic weight was not, in fact, the correct organising principle. In 1913, the English physicist Henry Moseley showed that the true ordering criterion was atomic number — the number of protons in the nucleus. This seemingly technical correction resolved several outstanding puzzles. Certain pairs of elements, notably tellurium and iodine, sat at positions that their atomic weights contradicted but that their chemical behaviour demanded; atomic number placed them correctly. Moseley's work also confirmed exactly how many elements could exist between hydrogen and uranium, removing the possibility that further natural elements remained hidden in the lighter range.

The gaps at the heavier end of the table, however, proved richer than anticipated. Elements 93 through 118 do not occur naturally on Earth — or if they do, only in vanishingly small quantities from natural nuclear processes. They have been produced instead in laboratories, synthesised by bombarding heavy atoms with smaller projectiles in particle accelerators. Some exist only for fractions of a second before decaying. Others, predicted by theory to occupy a so-called *island of stability*, may prove relatively long-lived once they are successfully produced in sufficient quantity. The most recent additions — nihonium, moscovium, tennessine, and oganesson — were officially recognised in 2016.

Modern chemistry can therefore claim to have completed, at least provisionally, the picture Mendeleev began. Whether further elements exist beyond element 118 remains an open question. Physical theory suggests an upper limit at which atomic nuclei become too unstable to hold together, even momentarily, but the exact location of that boundary is contested. What seems clear is that the tool Mendeleev devised from incomplete data has continued to guide discovery for over a century and a half, long after every other aspect of its scientific context has been transformed.

## Key Vocabulary

- **deliberate** (adj) — done on purpose; intentional.
- **recurred** (v) — happened again; appeared repeatedly.
- **indicated** (v) — pointed to; suggested.
- **asserted** (v) — claimed confidently.
- **isolated** (v) — in chemistry, separated from a mixture.
- **accommodate** (v) — to fit within or make room for.
- **residue** (n) — what is left after a process is completed.
- **refused to react** (phrase) — did not undergo chemical change.
- **profound** (adj) — very great; far-reaching.
- **contradicted** (v) — disagreed with; opposed.
- **vanishingly small** (phrase) — so small as to be almost nothing.
- **synthesised** (v) — artificially produced, especially in a laboratory.
- **bombarding** (v) — attacking with a constant stream of something.
- **decaying** (v) — in physics, breaking down radioactively.
- **provisionally** (adv) — for the time being; not finally.
