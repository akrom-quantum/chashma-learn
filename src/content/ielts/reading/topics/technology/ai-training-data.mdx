# The Data Problem in Artificial Intelligence

## Passage

Modern artificial intelligence systems owe much of their performance to a practice that has, until recently, attracted little attention outside technical circles: the use of vast quantities of text, images, and other data to train them. A contemporary language model may be exposed to the equivalent of millions of books during its initial training phase, learning from this corpus the patterns by which human language operates. Image-generation systems learn in the same way from billions of labelled photographs. The strength of these systems depends almost entirely on the scale and quality of the data they consume, and this has produced consequences that are only now becoming clear.

The first consequence is a bottleneck. The most capable models are trained on a substantial fraction of the publicly accessible internet — a resource that, while enormous, is not infinite. Researchers have begun to calculate when this pool of high-quality text will be fully utilised. Various estimates place the exhaustion point somewhere between 2026 and 2032, depending on how quickly model sizes continue to grow and how much of the remaining data is considered usable. After that point, simply adding more of the same kind of data will no longer be an option. Developers will need to generate new data synthetically, train on more specialised material, or accept that further gains from pure scale will become harder to achieve.

The second consequence is legal. A growing number of lawsuits have challenged the assumption that training an AI system on copyrighted material constitutes a permitted use. Authors, artists, and news publishers have argued that their work is effectively being copied, and that the resulting models compete commercially with the original creators. Courts have so far produced inconsistent rulings, and national approaches vary widely. Some jurisdictions have introduced explicit exceptions for text and data mining; others treat training as ordinary copying unless the rights holder has given permission. The eventual resolution of these disputes will shape what future models can be legally trained on, and where.

The third consequence is cultural. Systems trained predominantly on English-language material from Western-authored sources produce outputs that reflect those perspectives disproportionately. A model asked to describe a typical wedding, for instance, will tend to produce a description recognisable to American or British readers, even when the user has provided no such context. Efforts to make training data more linguistically and culturally diverse have progressed slowly, partly because sources in other languages are simply less available online, and partly because filtering and evaluating quality in unfamiliar languages is technically difficult. The result is a subtle but persistent skew in what these systems treat as normal.

A fourth concern has begun to attract attention only recently. As AI systems generate ever more of the content that circulates online, the next generation of models trained on scraped internet data increasingly learn from the outputs of previous models rather than from human-generated material. Researchers have described this phenomenon as model collapse — a gradual degradation in quality as each new generation absorbs the characteristic errors of the one before. Whether this effect can be mitigated by careful curation of training data, or whether it imposes a genuine limit on further improvement, is a question the field has yet to resolve.

These problems do not suggest that artificial intelligence is approaching a dead end. They do suggest that the straightforward scaling strategy of the 2020s — larger models trained on ever-larger datasets — is reaching natural limits and will need to be supplemented by more thoughtful approaches. What those approaches will look like, and who will bear the cost of developing them, are among the more consequential questions facing the field as it enters its next phase.

## Key Vocabulary

- **corpus** (n) — a collection of written or spoken texts used for analysis.
- **consume** (v) — in computing, to use up or process data.
- **bottleneck** (n) — a point of congestion or limitation in a process.
- **exhaustion point** (phrase) — the moment when something is completely used up.
- **synthetically** (adv) — artificially produced; not naturally occurring.
- **constitutes** (v) — amounts to; makes up.
- **jurisdictions** (n) — areas governed by particular legal systems.
- **predominantly** (adv) — for the most part; mainly.
- **disproportionately** (adv) — in an unbalanced way; too much relative to something else.
- **skew** (n) — a bias or distortion in a particular direction.
- **circulates** (v) — moves around; is shared widely.
- **scraped** (v) — in computing, extracted from websites automatically.
- **degradation** (n) — gradual decline in quality.
- **mitigated** (v) — made less severe.
- **curation** (n) — the careful selection and organisation of material.
- **supplemented** (v) — added to in order to improve.
