# The Lithium-Ion Revolution

## Passage

The device you are almost certainly holding, sitting near, or carrying contains a lithium-ion battery. Phones, laptops, electric vehicles, power tools, hearing aids, and grid-scale energy storage systems all depend on a chemistry that, until the late 1970s, barely existed outside laboratories. The 2019 Nobel Prize in Chemistry was awarded jointly to three researchers — John Goodenough, Stanley Whittingham, and Akira Yoshino — for their role in developing what is now arguably the most important energy-storage technology of the contemporary age.

A battery, in simple terms, is a device that releases stored chemical energy as electricity. Lithium proved a promising candidate for rechargeable batteries because it is the lightest metal and has a strong tendency to release electrons — both qualities that allow lithium batteries to store more energy per unit of weight than older alternatives. Whittingham built the first functional lithium battery in 1976, using titanium disulfide as one electrode. It was technically impressive but commercially impractical: the battery was prone to catching fire when disturbed. Goodenough made a critical improvement in 1980 by substituting cobalt oxide for titanium disulfide, doubling the voltage and greatly improving safety. Yoshino completed the breakthrough in 1985 by replacing pure lithium metal with a carbon material that could safely store lithium ions. The modern lithium-ion battery was born.

Sony commercialised the first lithium-ion battery in 1991, initially for use in camcorders. Within a decade the technology had displaced earlier rechargeable chemistries in nearly all consumer electronics. The reasons were straightforward: lithium-ion cells offered higher energy density, slower self-discharge when idle, and — crucially — no memory effect, the phenomenon by which older nickel-based batteries gradually lost capacity if repeatedly charged before being fully drained. Users could simply plug their devices in whenever convenient, a behavioural shift with far-reaching consequences for how portable electronics were designed and used.

The transition from consumer electronics to vehicles took longer. A laptop battery stores a few dozen watt-hours; an electric car requires tens of thousands. Scaling the chemistry required solving engineering problems that were largely invisible at smaller sizes: how to manage the heat produced during rapid charging, how to prevent thermal runaway if one cell in a pack failed, and how to reduce costs to a level competitive with internal combustion. Tesla's 2008 Roadster demonstrated that a luxury electric vehicle was technically feasible; it took another decade of sustained cost reductions before electric vehicles became plausible as mass-market products.

The cost trajectory has been remarkable. A lithium-ion battery pack that cost roughly 1,200 US dollars per kilowatt-hour in 2010 costs less than one-eighth that amount in recent estimates. These reductions have come from multiple sources: improvements in battery chemistry, larger and more automated factories, and reductions in the cost of key materials. The effects have cascaded beyond vehicles. Large-scale battery installations are now economically viable for storing energy from intermittent renewable sources — solar and wind generation produce power when conditions allow, not when demand peaks, and grid-scale batteries smooth the mismatch. Several regions that once considered battery storage prohibitively expensive now deploy it routinely as part of their power systems.

Challenges remain. The lithium-ion chemistry still depends on cobalt and nickel, both of which are mined under conditions that raise ethical and environmental concerns. Recycling infrastructure has lagged behind deployment, so that a growing stock of used batteries awaits processes that do not yet exist at scale. Alternative chemistries — sodium-ion, solid-state lithium, and various flow batteries — promise to reduce some of these issues but have yet to match lithium-ion's combination of performance and cost. For the foreseeable future, the technology that emerged from three separate laboratories in the 1970s and 1980s is likely to remain the dominant means by which humanity stores electricity.
