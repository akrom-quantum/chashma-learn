# The Quiet Influence of Maria Montessori

## Passage

Maria Montessori was in her late twenties when, in 1896, she became one of the first women to qualify as a physician in Italy. Her early work was with children classified as mentally deficient — a population her profession had largely written off as unteachable. Working from the observation that even severely disadvantaged children responded to structured sensory stimulation, Montessori developed teaching materials and methods that allowed many of her pupils to reach academic standards previously thought beyond them. Several of her students passed state examinations alongside children from ordinary schools. The result, widely publicised in the Italian press, prompted Montessori to ask a question that would occupy the rest of her career: if children with severe disadvantages could achieve what was previously thought impossible, what might ordinary children achieve if given similar conditions?

Her answer emerged gradually from practical experimentation. In 1907, Montessori was invited to oversee a childcare centre in one of Rome's poorest neighbourhoods, where working parents needed somewhere safe for their young children during the day. The centre, known as the *Casa dei Bambini*, became the first expression of what would become the Montessori method. Rather than a traditional classroom with a teacher addressing rows of seated pupils, the environment was organised around activities that children could choose and pursue independently. Furniture was sized for small bodies. Materials were designed to be self-correcting, so that a child could tell without adult intervention whether a task had been completed successfully. Teachers — whom Montessori preferred to call directors — observed quietly and intervened only when necessary.

Observers were startled by what happened. Children who might have been expected to demand constant attention instead concentrated for extended periods on activities they had chosen themselves. Disruptive behaviour, the ordinary accompaniment of childcare at scale, proved uncommon. Children who were given the opportunity to work with numbers or letters at their own pace often began reading and performing calculations at ages earlier than educational orthodoxy thought possible. Montessori, writing later about her own surprise at these results, argued that she had not taught the children anything directly; she had merely prepared an environment in which their natural inclinations could express themselves.

The method spread rapidly. By the 1920s, Montessori schools operated in Europe, North America, India, and elsewhere. The rise of authoritarian regimes in the 1930s proved disruptive — Mussolini initially supported Montessori schools in Italy but later closed them when Montessori refused to align her curriculum with fascist ideology. She spent the Second World War in India, where she had been giving lectures when travel became impossible. There she developed further the elementary stages of her method and trained thousands of teachers before her eventual return to Europe.

The scientific status of the method has been contested throughout. Critics have argued that Montessori's claims rested more on her own observations than on controlled studies, and that many of the effects attributed to her classrooms could be explained by selection effects — the kinds of families who enrol children in Montessori schools tend to differ from the general population in ways that would predict good outcomes under any method. Supporters have pointed to more recent controlled research, including a 2006 study published in the journal *Science*, which compared randomly selected children from a Montessori school with peers who applied but were placed, by lottery, in conventional schools. The Montessori group performed better on several academic and social measures, particularly at age five. Subsequent research has produced broadly similar results, though with considerable variation depending on how closely schools follow the original method.

What is clearer is Montessori's indirect influence. Elements of her approach — child-sized furniture, multi-age groupings, hands-on learning materials, emphasis on independent work — have become common in mainstream education, often without acknowledgement of their origin. The language early-childhood educators use to describe their practice is saturated with ideas Montessori introduced: sensitive periods, prepared environments, the role of concentration in development, the view of the child as an active constructor of their own learning rather than a passive recipient of instruction. Whether or not any given school today identifies as Montessori, the contemporary early-childhood classroom owes her a debt it rarely acknowledges explicitly.

- **oversee** (v) — to supervise.

- **inclinations** (n) — natural tendencies or preferences.
- **startled** (v) — surprised or shocked.
- **accompaniment** (n) — something that typically goes along with another thing.
- **orthodoxy** (n) — the established view.

- **authoritarian** (adj) — favouring strict obedience to authority.
- **curriculum** (n) — the set of courses offered by a school.

- **saturated with** (phrase) — full of; steeped in.
- **constructor** (n) — one who builds something.
- **debt it rarely acknowledges** (phrase) — an obligation seldom recognised publicly.
