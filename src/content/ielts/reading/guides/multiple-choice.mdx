# Multiple Choice

You're given a question or an incomplete statement and four options (A, B, C, D). Pick the one that matches the passage. Sometimes you get five options and must pick two, or seven options and must pick three — the instructions will tell you.

This looks like the easiest question type. It isn't. Three of the four options will look plausible, and at least two will use words directly from the passage to trap you.

---

## What's Being Tested

- Detailed understanding of a specific part of the passage.
- Your ability to reject **distractors** — options that sound right but aren't.
- Whether you're matching **meaning** or just matching **words**.

Questions appear in the **same order as the information in the passage**. Q21 comes before Q22 in the text. Use this.

---

## Strategy

**1. Read the question stem first. Underline the key noun.**
Not the whole question — the one thing being asked about. "According to the writer, the main reason for X was..." → underline X.

**2. Scan the passage for that noun or its synonym.**
Don't read. Scan. Your eye is looking for one thing.

**3. Read the sentence containing the noun, plus the sentence before and after.**
The answer is almost always in this three-sentence window.

**4. Cover the options. Predict the answer in your own words.**
This is the step 90% of students skip. If you read the options first, they colonize your thinking. Predict first, then match.

**5. Eliminate, don't select.**
Cross out options that are clearly wrong. When two remain, re-read the passage sentence. The correct answer will paraphrase it. The distractor will repeat its words while twisting the meaning.

---

## The Four Distractor Patterns

Memorize these. Every wrong answer is one of them.

> [!warning] Pattern 1 — Word match, meaning mismatch
> The option uses vocabulary directly from the passage but says something the passage doesn't.
> Passage: *"Solar panels have become cheaper, but installation costs remain high."*
> Trap option: *"Solar panels are expensive."* (The panels aren't — the installation is.)

> [!warning] Pattern 2 — Too extreme
> The option is right in spirit but uses absolute language (*always*, *never*, *all*, *impossible*) where the passage uses hedged language (*often*, *tends to*, *most*).

> [!warning] Pattern 3 — Partially true
> Half of the option matches the passage. The other half adds or changes something. One wrong word invalidates the whole option.

> [!warning] Pattern 4 — True, but not the answer
> The option is a true statement found in the passage — just not the answer to **this specific question**. Always re-check: "Does this answer what's actually being asked?"

## Worked Example

**Passage extract:**
*"The Roman road network, often cited as the foundation of European infrastructure, was built primarily for military purposes. Though merchants later benefited enormously from these roads, Roman engineers designed them to allow legions to move quickly between provinces, not to stimulate trade."*

**Question:** According to the passage, why were Roman roads originally built?

- A. To help merchants transport goods across Europe.
- B. To establish Europe's infrastructure.
- C. To enable rapid movement of military forces.
- D. To connect distant Roman provinces.

**Working through it:**
- **A** — *Pattern 4*. True (merchants did benefit) but the passage explicitly says this was *later*, not the original purpose. Rejected.
- **B** — *Pattern 2*. "Foundation of European infrastructure" appears in the passage, but the phrase describes what the roads became, not why they were built. Rejected.
- **D** — *Pattern 3*. The roads did connect provinces, but the passage says movement between provinces was for **legions** — the province-connection is a consequence, not the purpose. Too vague.
- **C** — Correct. Paraphrases "allow legions to move quickly between provinces."

---

## Time Budget

60–90 seconds per question. If you're at 90 seconds and still torn between two options, pick the one closer to your original prediction and move on. You'll lose more marks agonizing than you will guessing.

---

## Common Mistakes

- **Reading all options before reading the passage.** You prime yourself to accept distractors.
- **Picking the option with the most passage-like vocabulary.** That's usually a trap.
- **Changing your answer at the end.** Your first, prediction-based instinct is right more often than your second-guess.
- **Getting stuck on one question.** Multiple Choice is worth one mark. So is every other question. Don't spend three minutes here.

> [!tip] The 2-out-of-5 and 3-out-of-7 variants
> When asked to pick two (or three) answers, each correct choice is worth one mark independently. If you get one right and one wrong, you get one mark — not zero. Never leave these blank.
