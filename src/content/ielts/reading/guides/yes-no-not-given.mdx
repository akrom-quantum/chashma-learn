# Yes / No / Not Given

Mechanically identical to [[true-false-not-given|True/False/Not Given]]. One critical difference: the statements test **the writer's opinions, claims, or arguments** — not objective facts about the world.

- **Yes** — the statement agrees with the writer's views.
- **No** — the statement contradicts the writer's views.
- **Not Given** — the writer doesn't express a view on this.

Appears on passages that are **discursive** — where a writer argues a position. Not every test has a YNG section; those that do usually pair it with a passage on a contested topic (education policy, environmental debates, historical interpretation).

---

## What Changes from TFNG

Everything about finding and matching statements works the same way. The difference is in **what you're looking for**:

| TFNG | YNG |
|---|---|
| Match factual claims | Match opinions and arguments |
| "The experiment used 500 subjects" | "The experiment was poorly designed" |
| Looks for dates, numbers, events | Looks for evaluative language |
| Distinguishes fact from silence | Distinguishes writer's view from reported views |

---

## Finding the Writer's Opinion

The writer's opinion is signaled by **evaluative** and **stance** language. Train your eye to catch these:

**Evaluative adjectives and adverbs:**
*flawed, compelling, misguided, remarkable, insufficient, unfortunately, crucially, strikingly*

**Stance verbs:**
*argues, claims, insists, dismisses, rejects, advocates, warns, contends*

**Hedging for the writer's own view:**
*I would argue, it seems clear that, one might reasonably conclude, the evidence suggests*

**Attribution for other people's views (which is NOT the writer's opinion):**
*Smith claims, according to researchers, critics argue, some have suggested*

> [!warning] The attribution trap
> If the passage says *"Critics argue that standardised testing harms creativity,"* and the statement is *"Standardised testing harms creativity,"* the answer is probably **Not Given** — because the writer reported what critics say without endorsing it. Look for whether the writer then agrees, disagrees, or stays neutral.

---

## Strategy

Same as TFNG, with one extra step:

**1. Read the statement. Ask: "Whose opinion is this claiming to be?"**
YNG statements are always about what **the writer** thinks. If the statement is about what someone else thinks, you need to check whether the writer shares that view.

**2. Find the relevant passage section.**

**3. Identify who is speaking in that section.**
Is the writer stating their own position, or reporting someone else's?

**4. Apply the Y / N / NG test.**

## Worked Examples

**Passage extract:**
*"Proponents of online learning argue that it democratises education by removing geographical barriers. This claim, while appealing, overlooks a harder truth: students without reliable internet access or quiet study environments remain excluded. Online education does not eliminate inequality — it merely redraws the lines along which inequality operates. Governments that rush to digital-first strategies risk entrenching the very divides they claim to close."*

**1. The writer believes online learning is a genuine equaliser.**
- The writer says proponents make this claim but calls it "appealing" then refutes it. The writer's view is the opposite. **No.**

**2. The writer agrees with governments that prioritise digital-first strategies.**
- The writer warns these governments "risk entrenching the very divides they claim to close." Clearly disagrees. **No.**

**3. The writer thinks online learning should be abolished.**
- The writer criticises overselling online learning and calls out the inequality argument. But "should be abolished" is much stronger than anything in the passage. The writer's position is nuanced critique, not abolition. **Not Given.**

**4. The writer considers inequality in education a serious concern.**
- The writer frames online education as "redrawing lines of inequality" and calls out the worsening of "divides." Concern about inequality underpins the whole argument. **Yes.**

## Common Mistakes

- **Confusing reported views with the writer's view.** "X argues Y" does not mean the writer believes Y.
- **Looking for factual contradictions.** YNG is about opinions, not facts. Something can be the writer's opinion without being "true."
- **Ignoring tone.** Sarcasm, scare quotes, and phrases like *so-called* signal disagreement even without an explicit refutation.
- **Writing Y/N/NG instead of Yes/No/Not Given.** Write the full word. Always.

> [!tip] Quick check before answering
> Ask yourself: *"If I asked the writer over coffee, would they agree with this statement?"* If yes → Yes. If they'd push back → No. If they'd shrug and say *"I never said"* → Not Given.
