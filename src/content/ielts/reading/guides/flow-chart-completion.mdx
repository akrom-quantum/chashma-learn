# Flow-chart Completion

A diagram showing a process or sequence — boxes connected by arrows — with some stages blank. Fill them in order, using exact words from the passage, within a stated word limit.

Flow-charts appear whenever the passage describes a **process**: manufacturing steps, scientific procedures, biological cycles, historical chains of cause-and-effect. Because processes are inherently sequential, flow-charts follow passage order strictly.

---

## What's Being Tested

- Understanding of cause-effect and step-by-step sequences.
- Ability to identify where one stage ends and the next begins.
- Exact-word transcription within a word limit.

Gaps are in **strict passage order** — the first blank is earlier in the passage than the second, and so on.

---

## Strategy

**1. Read the flow-chart first.**
Understand the overall process. Look at the filled boxes — they're landmarks. The boxes show you what the process starts with and ends with.

**2. Find the start of the process in the passage.**
Usually marked by phrases like "first," "the process begins with," "initially," "to start." The passage may describe the process in a single section, often signalled by sequence language (*then, next, following this, subsequently*).

**3. Read that section linearly. Track each stage.**

**4. When you hit a blank, identify what fills it based on the passage sentence.**

**5. Check word limit. Write the exact word(s).**

---

## Recognizing Sequence Language

Process passages use predictable markers. Scan for them:

| Function | Markers |
|---|---|
| Start | *first, initially, the process begins, to begin with* |
| Next step | *then, next, following this, subsequently, after this, the next stage* |
| Simultaneous | *meanwhile, at the same time, while* |
| Cause | *this causes, this results in, leading to, which produces* |
| Final step | *finally, ultimately, the end result, the process concludes with* |

Each marker signals a possible stage boundary in the flow-chart.

## Worked Example

**Passage extract:**
*"The manufacture of modern paper begins with debarking, where harvested logs are stripped of their outer bark by rotating drums. The debarked wood is then chipped into small pieces approximately 3 cm in length. These chips are transferred to a digester, where they are cooked with chemicals — typically sodium hydroxide — at high pressure to break down the lignin binding the cellulose fibres. The resulting pulp is washed thoroughly to remove residual chemicals, then bleached to produce white paper. The bleached pulp is mixed with water and fed into the paper machine, where it is spread onto a moving mesh. Water drains through the mesh, and the fibres mat together to form a continuous sheet. The wet sheet passes through heated rollers that press and dry it, producing finished paper ready for cutting and rolling."*

**Instructions: NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS from the passage.**

**Flow-chart:**

Logs harvested → **(1) ___** (rotating drums remove bark) → Wood **(2) ___** (into ~3 cm pieces) → Sent to **(3) ___** → Cooked with **(4) ___** → Pulp washed → Pulp **(5) ___** → Mixed with water → Spread on **(6) ___** → Water drains → Sheet passes through heated **(7) ___** → Finished paper

**Working through it:**

1. *First stage — bark removed* → passage: "begins with debarking." Answer: **debarking**.
2. *Wood processed into pieces* → passage: "chipped into small pieces." Answer: **chipped**.
3. *Destination of chips* → passage: "transferred to a digester." Answer: **digester** (or *a digester*; prefer just "digester" — 1 word).
4. *Cooking agent* → passage: "sodium hydroxide." Answer: **sodium hydroxide** (2 words).
5. *After washing, before mixing* → passage: "then bleached." Answer: **bleached**.
6. *Where pulp is spread* → passage: "spread onto a moving mesh." Answer: **moving mesh** (2 words) — or just **mesh** if word-limit tightness pushes you there. Either works; "moving mesh" matches the passage exactly.
7. *Press and dry mechanism* → passage: "heated rollers." Answer: **rollers** (1 word) — "heated" is already shown in the flow-chart's adjective, so "rollers" alone is right. Watch for this: the chart's text may already include part of the phrase.

---

## Time Budget

30–45 seconds per gap. Flow-charts are usually concentrated in one passage section, so once you find it, everything is sequential and fast.

---

## Common Mistakes

- **Losing track of the process section.** If you wander away from the passage's process paragraph, you'll guess. Stay inside the process description.
- **Missing a stage.** Passages sometimes compress two stages into one sentence: "The pulp is washed and bleached" is two stages. Count carefully.
- **Writing passage words that duplicate flow-chart text.** If the box already says "heated ___" and the passage says "heated rollers," write "rollers" — not "heated rollers."
- **Writing the wrong grammatical form.** If the box pattern is gerund-based (*washing, bleaching, drying*) and the passage uses noun forms (*wash, bleach, dry*), you may need to match the passage unless instructions say otherwise. Passage wording wins.

> [!tip] The filled-boxes trick
> Filled boxes in a flow-chart often mark the approximate midpoint or endpoints of the process description in the passage. If box 3 is filled and you're looking for box 2, the answer is between box 1's passage location and box 3's.
