# Matching Features

You're given a list of statements (usually 5–7) and a short list of features (usually 3–5 — people's names, country names, time periods, theories, etc.). Match each statement to one feature.

Typical setup: *"Match each view to the researcher who expressed it."* You have six views and four researchers. Some researchers match multiple views; one might match none. Instructions specify whether features can be reused.

---

## What's Being Tested

- Ability to attribute claims to their correct source in a passage that discusses multiple parties.
- Tracking who said what across a long, name-dense text.
- Distinguishing between similar-sounding positions held by different people.

Statements are **not in passage order**. Features in the feature list are also not in passage order.

---

## Strategy

**1. Scan the passage and mark every feature with a symbol.**
If the features are four researchers — Hill, Okafor, Santos, Vance — circle their names every time they appear in the passage. This takes 30 seconds and saves minutes later. It turns the passage into a map.

**2. Read the first statement. Identify the claim.**
*"Believes that the data was misinterpreted."* → I'm looking for someone who disputes an interpretation.

**3. Jump to the places where researchers are mentioned.**
Don't re-read the passage top to bottom. Go directly to the marked names and read those sentences.

**4. Match the claim to the researcher whose mentioned view fits.**

**5. Mark the match; continue.**

---

## Tracking Who Said What

The passage will distinguish between researchers using specific verbs and constructions. Read carefully:

| Language | What it signals |
|---|---|
| "Smith argues that…" | Smith's view — direct attribution |
| "According to Jones…" | Jones's view |
| "For Chen, the issue is…" | Chen's view |
| "Smith, however, disagrees" | Smith disagrees with the previous person |
| "Unlike Jones, Santos believes…" | Santos's view, contrasted with Jones |
| "Both Smith and Chen maintain…" | Shared view — either could match this statement if reuse is allowed |
| "Smith later revised this position, suggesting…" | Smith's updated view — be careful not to attribute the old one |

> [!warning] Pronoun traps
> A paragraph may open with "Smith argued that X" and then use "he" for three more sentences. All those "he" sentences are still Smith. If a new name appears mid-paragraph, track whether the subsequent pronouns refer to the new person or the old one. Pronoun confusion is the #1 source of wrong answers here.

## Worked Example

**Features:**
- A. Dr. Patel
- B. Dr. Rhee
- C. Dr. Sullivan
- D. Dr. Yamada

**Passage extract:**
*"Studies on language acquisition have produced conflicting interpretations. Dr. Patel, drawing on data from bilingual households in Toronto, concluded that children exposed to two languages before age three show measurable delays in vocabulary development in each language individually. Dr. Rhee contested this view, arguing that while individual-language vocabulary is smaller, the combined bilingual vocabulary exceeds that of monolingual peers — and that measuring one language in isolation is misleading. Dr. Sullivan's work added a temporal dimension: any delays observed in early childhood disappear entirely by age seven. Dr. Yamada, whose research focused on older learners, remained neutral on the childhood debate but demonstrated that adults who acquire a second language after thirty rarely achieve native-like pronunciation, regardless of study duration."*

**Statements:**
1. Believes that apparent early disadvantages correct themselves over time.
2. Argues that measuring bilingual children's vocabulary one language at a time produces misleading results.
3. Focuses on the limitations of later-in-life language learning.
4. Found evidence of slower vocabulary growth in early bilingual children.
5. Does not take a position on whether bilingualism affects young children.

**Working through it:**

1. *"Apparent early disadvantages correct themselves over time"* — Sullivan says delays "disappear entirely by age seven." Match: **C.**
2. *"Measuring one language at a time is misleading"* — Rhee explicitly says this. Match: **B.**
3. *"Limitations of later-in-life learning"* — Yamada focuses on adult learners after 30. Match: **D.**
4. *"Slower vocabulary growth in early bilingual children"* — Patel found "measurable delays in vocabulary development." Match: **A.**
5. *"Does not take a position on whether bilingualism affects young children"* — Yamada "remained neutral on the childhood debate." Match: **D.**

**Answers:** 1-C, 2-B, 3-D, 4-A, 5-D.

Note that D (Yamada) matches two statements. This is common when features can be reused — watch for a researcher whose views appear in multiple places.

---

## Time Budget

60–90 seconds per statement once the passage is mapped. The upfront 30-second scan-and-mark pays back several times over.

---

## Common Mistakes

- **Ignoring the feature list length.** If there are 4 features and 6 statements, reuse is required. Check the instructions.
- **Matching on general topic instead of specific view.** Two researchers might both work on "language acquisition." Match the specific claim.
- **Getting tripped by pronouns.** "He argued" — which he? Go back to the last named researcher.
- **Missing contrast markers.** "However," "in contrast," and "by contrast" flip the attribution. Read them carefully.

> [!tip] When none of the features seem to fit
> The statement is probably paraphrased heavily. Look for synonyms: "delays disappear" becomes "disadvantages correct themselves." If you still can't find a match, the statement's answer might rely on something said **implicitly** — check for tone and implication, not just direct claims.
