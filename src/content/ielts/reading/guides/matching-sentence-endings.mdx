# Matching Sentence Endings

You're given the first half of several sentences and a list of possible endings. Match each beginning to the correct ending so that the completed sentence reflects the passage accurately. There are always **more endings than beginnings** — at least two endings are distractors.

This type rewards two things: careful reading of grammar (the ending must grammatically fit the beginning) and accurate recall of what the passage actually said.

---

## What's Being Tested

- Detailed comprehension of specific claims in the passage.
- Attention to grammatical agreement and logical flow.
- Ability to distinguish genuine passage claims from plausible-sounding distractors.

Sentence beginnings appear in the **same order as information in the passage**. Endings in the ending list are **not** in any particular order.

---

## Strategy

**1. Read the first sentence beginning. Underline the key concept.**
*"The main reason that ancient Egyptians built pyramids was…"* → the key concept is "reason for pyramid construction."

**2. Scan the passage for that concept.**
Find the relevant section.

**3. Predict the ending from the passage, in your own words.**
Don't look at the ending options yet.

**4. Match your prediction to the ending list.**
The closest match is usually the answer.

**5. Verify grammatically.**
Read the full completed sentence (beginning + ending) aloud in your head. Does it sound like correct English? Wrong endings often don't fit grammatically.

**6. Verify against the passage.**
Does the completed sentence match what the passage actually says, not just sound reasonable?

---

## The Two Filters

Every wrong ending fails at least one of two tests:

> [!abstract] Grammar filter
> The ending must fit grammatically with the beginning. *"The researchers concluded that…"* needs a clause. *"The researchers were known for…"* needs a noun or gerund. Endings that fit the wrong sentence grammatically can be ruled out on form alone.

> [!abstract] Meaning filter
> Among the endings that fit grammatically, only one will match what the passage actually says. Others may be true statements, statements the passage contradicts, or statements the passage doesn't address — classic [[true-false-not-given|True/False/Not Given]]–style distractors.

Apply grammar filter first — it's faster and eliminates options quickly.

---

## Worked Example

**Passage extract:**
*"Japanese tea ceremony, or chanoyu, developed from the 9th-century practice of monks drinking tea to stay awake during meditation. By the 16th century, the tea master Sen no Rikyū had codified the ceremony into a formal art, emphasising simplicity, rustic beauty, and the concept of 'ichi-go ichi-e' — the idea that each meeting is unique and will never be repeated. Rikyū's influence persisted even after his forced suicide in 1591, and his three grandsons each founded schools of tea that continue today. Modern practitioners maintain that the ceremony is not merely ritualised hospitality but a spiritual practice demanding years of study."*

**Sentence beginnings:**
1. Tea was originally consumed by Japanese monks in order to…
2. Sen no Rikyū transformed the tea ceremony by…
3. The continuing influence of Rikyū is evident in…
4. Modern practitioners believe the tea ceremony requires…

**Ending options:**
- A. establishing formal principles such as simplicity and rustic beauty.
- B. the three schools of tea founded by his grandsons.
- C. prevent drowsiness during religious practice.
- D. years of dedicated study as a spiritual discipline.
- E. producing tea of higher quality than their contemporaries.
- F. replacing older Chinese tea traditions.

**Working through it:**

1. *"Tea was originally consumed by Japanese monks in order to…"* — Passage says "to stay awake during meditation." Match: **C** (prevent drowsiness during religious practice).

2. *"Sen no Rikyū transformed the tea ceremony by…"* — Passage says he "codified the ceremony into a formal art, emphasising simplicity, rustic beauty…" Match: **A** (establishing formal principles such as simplicity and rustic beauty).

3. *"The continuing influence of Rikyū is evident in…"* — Passage says "his three grandsons each founded schools of tea that continue today." Match: **B** (the three schools of tea founded by his grandsons).

4. *"Modern practitioners believe the tea ceremony requires…"* — Passage says "a spiritual practice demanding years of study." Match: **D** (years of dedicated study as a spiritual discipline).

**Distractors:** E (quality of tea) and F (replacing Chinese traditions) are unused — both are not-given content.

**Answers:** 1-C, 2-A, 3-B, 4-D.

---

## Grammar Hint Examples

Grammar can rule out options quickly. Examples of mismatches that would disqualify an ending:

- Beginning *"The scientists believed…"* needs a *that*-clause → endings starting with nouns fail.
- Beginning *"Darwin was known for…"* needs a gerund or noun → endings starting with *that* fail.
- Beginning *"The city grew because of…"* needs a noun phrase → endings starting with a subject+verb fail.

Always read the full completed sentence before committing.

---

## Time Budget

60 seconds per match. This is a faster question type than most — grammar filtering eliminates half the options quickly, and passage order keeps you linear. Don't over-invest.

---

## Common Mistakes

- **Ignoring grammar.** A grammatically wrong ending is never the right answer.
- **Matching on topic keywords.** Two endings might both mention "schools" — only one will correctly complete this specific sentence.
- **Forgetting passage order.** The beginnings go in passage order. If you match beginning #3 to a paragraph earlier than the match for beginning #2, one of them is wrong.
- **Picking an ending that's "probably true."** The ending has to be what the **passage says** — not what's true in general.

> [!tip] Last-sentence rescue
> If you're stuck between two endings for one beginning, check which endings you've already matched for other beginnings. Used endings can't be answers (unless instructions say endings can be reused — rare). Elimination narrows it faster than re-reading.
