# Matching Headings

You're given a list of headings (usually 8–10, numbered i, ii, iii…) and asked to assign one to each paragraph of the passage. There are always **more headings than paragraphs** — at least two will be unused distractors.

This is the single hardest question type for most test-takers. It tests whether you can identify the **main idea** of a paragraph — not any one thing the paragraph contains, but its overall point. Score improvement on Matching Headings moves your band more than any other single skill.

---

## What's Being Tested

- Paragraph-level main-idea identification (not sentence-level, not passage-level).
- Ability to distinguish the **main idea** from **supporting details**.
- Resistance to surface-level word matching (the primary distractor technique).

Unlike most question types, **Matching Headings does not follow passage order** mechanically — you'll match headings to paragraphs A, B, C in whatever order you solve them.

---

## Why It's Hard

The trap is almost always the same: a heading will repeat a prominent word or phrase from the paragraph — but that word appears in a **supporting detail**, not the main idea. Your eye catches the word. Your brain says "match." You lose the mark.

**Example of the trap:**

> Paragraph: *"Recent studies have transformed how scientists understand memory formation. By tracking individual neurons, researchers have shown that memory is not stored in a single location but distributed across networks. This finding overturns decades of assumptions. The next challenge is understanding how these distributed networks coordinate — a problem that will occupy neuroscience for years to come."*

> Heading A: *"The role of individual neurons"*
> Heading B: *"A shift in the understanding of memory"*
> Heading C: *"Future challenges in neuroscience"*

All three headings touch something in the paragraph. Only B captures the paragraph's **main point**. A is a method detail. C is a forward-looking sentence at the end — true but peripheral. Students who scan often pick A or C.

---

## Strategy

**1. Read all the headings first. Slowly.**
Underline the distinctive word in each — the word that differentiates this heading from the others. If two headings both mention "education," the distinctive word in each is the other word.

**2. Read the first paragraph.**
Don't scan — read. You need the meaning, not the words.

**3. Close your eyes. Summarise the paragraph in one short sentence.**
What was it about? Not what words appeared — what was the **point**?

**4. Match your summary to the headings.**
The correct heading paraphrases your summary. The wrong headings paraphrase individual sentences from the paragraph.

**5. Move to the next paragraph. Repeat.**

**6. When two paragraphs seem to match the same heading, re-read both.**
One is right; the other matches a different heading you overlooked.

---

## The Main-Idea Test

Ask these three questions for each paragraph:

1. **What is the paragraph about?** (subject)
2. **What does the paragraph say about the subject?** (claim)
3. **Why is this paragraph here in the passage?** (function)

The correct heading answers 1 and 2 together. Distractors usually answer just 1.

---

## Identifying Main Ideas

Main ideas tend to live in specific places:

- **First sentence.** Topic sentence, especially in academic writing. Often — but not always — the main idea.
- **Last sentence.** Concluding summary, particularly in argumentative paragraphs.
- **After a transition.** "However," "In contrast," "More importantly," "The real question" — these often mark the pivot to the actual main idea.
- **Across the paragraph.** Sometimes the main idea isn't in any single sentence — it's what the paragraph **adds up to**.

> [!warning] Don't trust the first sentence blindly
> In sophisticated academic writing, the first sentence is often a common-sense setup that the rest of the paragraph **qualifies, complicates, or overturns**. Read to the end before committing.

## Worked Example

**Headings list:**
- i. The origins of early trade networks
- ii. A surprising source of ancient wealth
- iii. How salt was harvested in prehistoric times
- iv. The decline of traditional salt production
- v. Salt's role in shaping political power
- vi. Religious ceremonies involving salt
- vii. Why salt lost its economic value

**Paragraph A:**
*"Before refrigeration, salt was the primary means of preserving meat and fish, making it indispensable to any society that ate more than it could eat fresh. Cities grew around salt deposits. Wars were fought over access to salt mines. Roman soldiers were partly paid in salt — the origin of the word 'salary.' Taxation on salt funded empires and sparked revolutions: the French gabelle and the British salt tax in colonial India both became symbols of tyranny. For much of history, the person who controlled the salt controlled the state."*

**Reading it properly:**
- Subject: salt.
- Claim: controlling salt meant controlling political power.
- Evidence: wars, taxes, revolutions, salary etymology.

**Checking each heading:**
- **i** (origins of trade networks) — Salt is economic, but the paragraph isn't about trade network origins. Distractor.
- **ii** (surprising source of ancient wealth) — Tempting. "Surprising" isn't quite right though — the whole paragraph frames salt's importance as historically central, not surprising.
- **iii** (how it was harvested) — No harvesting method mentioned. Distractor.
- **iv** (decline of traditional production) — No decline mentioned. Distractor.
- **v** (salt's role in shaping political power) — Directly captures "controlled salt → controlled state" and all the supporting evidence (wars, taxes, revolutions).
- **vi** (religious ceremonies) — Not mentioned.
- **vii** (lost economic value) — Opposite of the paragraph's point.

**Answer: v.**

> [!tip] Why ii was the trap
> A rushed test-taker might pick ii because "wealth" feels related to salt's importance. But the paragraph is about **political power**, not wealth — and certainly not about **surprise**. The most-tempting wrong answer is almost always a partial match.

---

## Time Budget

This is the slowest type. Budget ~2.5 minutes per paragraph. If the passage has 7 paragraphs and 7 headings needed, that's 17 minutes — nearly the entire passage's time allocation. Consider doing Matching Headings **before** reading the rest of the passage in detail, since understanding main ideas helps you answer later questions faster.

---

## Common Mistakes

- **Matching on one keyword.** The biggest error. Headings are designed to share vocabulary with distractor paragraphs.
- **Picking the first reasonable match.** Read all headings, finish all paragraphs, then commit. Often your initial match belongs to a different paragraph.
- **Forgetting distractors exist.** 8 headings and 5 paragraphs means 3 headings are never used. Don't force a weak match because "every heading should fit somewhere" — they shouldn't.
- **Re-reading the paragraph instead of re-reading the heading.** If you're stuck, the heading's wording is usually where the clarity lives.

> [!danger] The example paragraph trap
> IELTS often provides a **sample paragraph matched for you** in the question stem: *"Paragraph B has been done for you as an example."* Cross that heading off your list immediately — it cannot be the answer to any other paragraph. Students forget this and waste time considering it.
