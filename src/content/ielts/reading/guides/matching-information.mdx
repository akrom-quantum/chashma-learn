# Matching Information

You're given a list of statements (e.g. *"an example of how X led to Y,"* *"a description of a failed experiment"*) and asked which paragraph of the passage contains each one. Paragraphs are labelled A, B, C, D…

Critically: **questions are NOT in the same order as the passage.** This is one of only two question types that breaks the standard ordering rule (the other being Matching Headings). That's what makes this type slow — you can't move linearly.

Also critically: **a paragraph may be used more than once, or not at all.** The instructions will tell you which.

---

## What's Being Tested

- Ability to identify **specific information** within a paragraph.
- Recognition of function — examples, reasons, comparisons, consequences — not just topic.
- Fast, non-linear scanning.

This question type tests whether you can say *"this paragraph contains an example of X,"* not just *"this paragraph is about X."* An example is different from a definition. A cause is different from an effect. Read the question stems carefully.

---

## Strategy

**1. Do this question type LAST.**
Or at least after any question types that follow passage order. You'll have already read the passage for those, and your familiarity makes matching faster.

**2. Read all the statements first. Underline the key concept in each.**
What kind of information are you hunting for?
- *"A reference to a common misconception"* → I'm looking for something the writer contradicts or corrects.
- *"An example of a scientific collaboration"* → I need a named example, not a general claim.
- *"A statistic that surprised researchers"* → I need a number + surprise language.

**3. Work paragraph by paragraph, not statement by statement.**
Read paragraph A. Ask: "Which of my statements does this paragraph contain?" Mark any matches. Move to paragraph B. This is faster than scanning the whole passage for each statement.

**4. Don't match on topic — match on function.**
If the question asks for *"an example,"* a paragraph that discusses the topic without giving a concrete example doesn't count.

---

## Recognising Information Types

The question stems use specific language. Train on these:

| Stem phrase | What to look for |
|---|---|
| "an example of…" | A named case, specific instance, concrete illustration |
| "a reason for…" | Causal language: *because, due to, caused by, owing to* |
| "a reference to…" | Any mention, however brief — lowest bar |
| "a comparison between…" | Two things contrasted: *whereas, unlike, similarly, in contrast* |
| "a description of…" | Detailed explanation, not just a name |
| "a prediction about…" | Future-tense claim: *will, expected to, likely to, projected* |
| "a criticism of…" | Negative evaluation: *flawed, inadequate, problematic* |
| "an explanation of…" | Mechanism or reasoning: *this occurs because, the process works by* |
| "a suggestion that…" | Recommendation: *should, ought to, could consider* |

## Worked Example

**Passage with four paragraphs (extracts):**

> **A.** Before the invention of the refrigerator, preserving food relied on salting, smoking, and drying — methods that altered flavour and texture significantly. Ice harvesting from frozen lakes supplied wealthier households, but remained impractical for most.

> **B.** The first commercial refrigerator was developed in 1834 by Jacob Perkins, though his design saw little commercial success. It was not until Carl von Linde's 1876 patent that mechanical refrigeration became economically viable.

> **C.** Early 20th-century refrigerators used toxic gases like ammonia and sulphur dioxide as refrigerants. Household accidents were common, and in 1929 a single refrigerant leak in a Cleveland hospital killed more than one hundred people.

> **D.** Modern hydrofluorocarbon refrigerants have been criticised for their contribution to global warming. Climate scientists warn that continued reliance on these compounds could undo decades of emissions progress.

**Questions:**
1. An example of a fatal accident involving refrigeration.
2. A reference to early food preservation techniques.
3. A concern about environmental impact.
4. A comparison between two inventors.

**Working through paragraph by paragraph:**

- **A** contains food preservation methods → matches Q2.
- **B** mentions both Perkins and von Linde → is this a *comparison*? No, it describes them sequentially without contrasting them. **Q4 is not B.** In fact, none of the paragraphs directly compare inventors, so Q4's answer may be "no paragraph" if that's allowed, or we need to re-read. Re-checking B: Perkins's design had "little commercial success" while von Linde's was "economically viable" — that **is** a comparison by contrast. **Q4 = B.**
- **C** describes the 1929 Cleveland accident → matches Q1.
- **D** discusses environmental criticism → matches Q3.

**Answers:** 1-C, 2-A, 3-D, 4-B.

> [!warning] Q4 is the instructive one
> On first read, B looks like neutral history. The comparison is implicit — one inventor failed, another succeeded — and you have to notice the contrast to catch it. This is typical Matching Information difficulty: the match is real but requires inference about function.

---

## Time Budget

90–120 seconds per statement, but because you work paragraph-by-paragraph, budget by paragraph instead: ~2 minutes per paragraph for a 5-paragraph passage with 5 questions.

---

## Common Mistakes

- **Doing this question type first.** You waste time re-scanning paragraphs you'll re-read for other questions anyway.
- **Matching on topic words instead of function.** "Paragraph about coffee" isn't the same as "an example of coffee's economic impact."
- **Forgetting a paragraph can be used more than once.** If two statements both match paragraph C and the instructions allow reuse, answer C for both.
- **Skipping the check when a paragraph can be used more than once.** If reuse is allowed, at least one paragraph probably isn't used at all — and at least one is used twice.

> [!tip] Speed tactic
> When you find a match, lightly number the question against the paragraph in the test booklet. When you've matched 3 of 5, the remaining 2 statements are often process of elimination against paragraphs you haven't used yet.
